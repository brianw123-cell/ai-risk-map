# AI's energy and resource demand outpaces its gains

**In one sentence:** The build-out of AI data centers grows electricity, water and emissions faster than efficiency and clean energy can offset, locking in new fossil fuel plants and pushing costs onto households and local communities while the benefits go mainly to the companies building AI.

## Preconditions
- Demand for AI computing keeps growing faster than the energy used per task falls (the rebound effect, explained below).
- Clean generation and transmission can't be connected fast enough where data centers cluster, so natural gas fills the gap.
- Grid upgrade and capacity costs are spread across all customers rather than charged to the data centers that cause them.
- Promised climate benefits from AI stay unmeasured or small.

## Timing and reversibility
- **Earliest plausible date:** already under way in some places. US electricity prices rose 6.9% in 2025 against 2.9% overall inflation ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/12/electricity-price-data-center-ai-inflation-goldman.html)), and Google, Microsoft and Amazon all reported rising absolute emissions for 2025.
- **Speed:** slow-building but fast by energy-system standards. The International Energy Agency projects global data center electricity roughly doubling between 2025 and 2030.
- **Reversibility:** partly irreversible. A new gas plant runs for decades, and carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere for centuries. Prices and water use can recover if rules change or demand falls short.

## The case that this happens
*Short excerpts for orientation only. A person from this side will choose the final quotes at sign-off.*

- Sasha Luccioni (Hugging Face), Emma Strubell (Carnegie Mellon) and Kate Crawford argue in a [2025 FAccT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16548) that efficiency gains invite more use, and that "a narrow focus on direct emissions misrepresents AI's true climate footprint."
- Monitoring Analytics, the independent market monitor for PJM (the largest US grid region), identified data center load growth as the primary reason for record capacity prices that all customers in the region pay ([Utility Dive, January 2026](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/data-centers-pjm-capacity-auction/808951/)).

**Plain summary (needs sign-off from this side):** The efficiency gains are real, and total use is rising anyway, which is exactly what Jevons paradox predicts (in 1865 the economist William Stanley Jevons noticed that more efficient steam engines made Britain burn more coal, not less). Google, Microsoft and Amazon each reported double-digit emissions increases for 2025, and Meta quit a major renewable energy pledge after funding new gas plants. Hundreds of gigawatts of new gas capacity are being planned, much of it for data centers. Households in the largest US grid region are already paying billions more because of expected data center demand, and residents near data centers are fighting over water. The climate benefits AI companies promise are mostly unverified and arrive later, while the emissions happen now.

## The case that it doesn't
- Bill Gates said in [June 2024](https://www.eceee.org/all-news/news/ai-will-be-help-rather-than-hindrance-in-hitting-climate-targets-bill-gates-says/) that data centers would add at most about 6% to energy demand and probably 2% to 2.5%, that AI will more than offset it, and: "Let's not go overboard on this." (secondary)
- Jonathan Koomey, a longtime researcher of computing's energy use, warns regulators in a [March 2026 report with Gridlab](https://www.koomey.com/koomey_blog/new-report-out-today-separating-fact-from-fiction-in-data-center-electricity-forecasts-a-guide-for-regulators/) that data center forecasts are "highly uncertain" and that analysts habitually overestimate information technology's energy effects.

**Plain summary (needs sign-off from this side):** Data centers that do computing are about 1.5% of the world's electricity. The energy used per AI answer is tiny and falling faster than for any energy technology in history, according to the International Energy Agency. Many announced projects will never be built, so forecasts based on interconnection requests overstate demand, as they did in the dot-com era. Meanwhile the grid is changing quickly: the US is on track for a record year of new capacity, mostly solar and batteries, and the existing grid can absorb far more demand if data centers agree to cut use in the few peak hours each year. States are already making data centers pay their own way. The real constraint is how fast power can be connected, not whether it exists.

## Common ground
- Data center electricity use is rising fast, and the 2025 figures are measured, not projected.
- The energy per AI query is small and falling quickly; individual use is not where this is decided.
- The largest AI companies' absolute emissions rose in 2025 despite their climate goals.
- Long-range demand forecasts are very uncertain, and some proposed projects will not be built.
- In principle, new large customers should pay for the grid upgrades built for them; the disagreement is over the details.
- Better public data on data center energy and water use would help everyone.

## Cruxes
1. **Where data centers cluster, does clean power keep pace, or does gas fill the gap?** If gas-fired capacity built for data centers keeps growing faster than clean capacity in those regions, the worried side is right about lock-in. *Check: Global Energy Monitor tracker and EIA capacity data, 2027.*
2. **Do the new large-load tariffs stop costs shifting to households?** *Check: Virginia's new data center rate class in its first year (2027) and the next PJM capacity auction results (mid-2027).*
3. **Do verified AI-enabled emissions cuts appear at a scale comparable to data center emissions?** *Check: IEA Energy and AI updates, 2027.*

## Evidence confidence
**Medium.** Electricity use, prices, corporate emissions and gas build-out are measured and well documented for 2025. Whether demand will keep outpacing clean supply and efficiency through 2030, and how large AI's offsetting benefits are, is genuinely uncertain.

## Signposts
1. Google, Microsoft or Amazon reports another rise in absolute emissions in its 2027 sustainability report (covering 2026), showing 2025 was a trend rather than a peak.
2. Solar and batteries fall below half of planned new US generating capacity in an EIA annual outlook (they were 79% of planned 2026 additions).
3. The next PJM capacity auction again clears at its price cap with the market monitor attributing 40% or more of the cost to data centers.
4. US electricity prices rise faster than overall inflation again in 2026 (in 2025 they rose 6.9% against 2.9%).
5. A public water authority restricts household water use and names data center demand as a cause.

## Evidence so far
- **Electricity:** data centers worldwide used roughly 485 TWh in 2025, with AI-focused facilities growing about 50% in the year ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). An IEA presentation in June 2026 said data center demand grew by almost 20% in 2025, and that 19 of the 21 large AI data centers under construction in the US are within 65 km of a city of at least 100,000 people ([IEA, 11 June 2026](https://asiacleanenergyforum.adb.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Key-questions-on-energy-and-AI.pdf)).
- **Company emissions (2025, reported mid-2026):** Google's electricity use rose 37% and its emissions 18%, now more than 80% above its 2019 baseline; it calls its 2030 goal "intentionally aspirational" ([Trellis](https://trellis.net/article/googles-ai-expansion-weighs-heavily-on-climate-goals/)) (secondary). Microsoft's emissions rose about 25%, to 20.3 million tonnes, about 58% above its 2020 baseline ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/microsoft-emissions-25-percent-ai-data-centres)) (secondary). Amazon's rose 16%, with electricity-related emissions up 34% ([ESG Dive](https://www.esgdive.com/news/amazon-2025-emissions-jump-as-ai-brings-momentum-and-complexity-report/)) (secondary).
- **Gas build-out:** Meta left the RE100 renewable electricity pledge in July 2026; the Climate Group said Meta could no longer meet its criteria "due to investments made in new gas power," including gas plants for its Hyperion campus in Louisiana ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/meta-leaves-re100-clean-energy-gas-ai-data-centres)) (secondary; reported plant counts vary). US gas capacity in development rose 50% in six months to 378 GW by mid-2026, and nearly two-thirds of Texas's pipeline (77 GW) is planned to power data centers directly ([Global Energy Monitor, August 2026](https://globalenergymonitor.org/research/us-gas-power-proposals-tied-data-centers-nearly-double-six-months)). Much of this may never be built.
- **Bills:** in PJM, the largest US grid region (mid-Atlantic and Midwest), the market monitor attributed $6.5 billion of a record $16.4 billion capacity auction (December 2025) to data centers, and $21.3 billion of $47.2 billion across the last three auctions ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/data-centers-pjm-capacity-auction/808951/)). Expected data center demand was "a primary reason" for $23 billion in customer price increases running through at least 2028 ([Fortune, July 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/07/14/data-centers-23-billion-electricity-bills/)). Utilities requested a record $31 billion in rate increases in 2025 ([PowerLines](https://powerlines.org/utilities-requested-record-31-billion-in-rate-increases-in-2025-double-that-of-2024/)).
- **Water:** Google's water consumption reached 10.9 billion gallons in 2025, up 34% ([Google 2026 Environmental Report](https://sustainability.google/google-2026-environmental-report/), per press coverage). A 2025 Bloomberg analysis found more than two-thirds of US data centers built or in development since 2022 are in drought-prone areas ([KUNC, June 2025](https://www.kunc.org/2025-06-02/ai-data-centers-growing-drought-stricken-mountain-west-analysis)) (secondary). Tucson's council rejected the Amazon-linked Project Blue over water and energy concerns in August 2025 ([AZFamily](https://www.azfamily.com/2025/08/09/tucson-rejects-amazon-linked-data-center-amid-water-energy-concerns/)); it is now being built outside city limits on state-approved wells and could use up to 31 million gallons a year ([KJZZ, May 2026](https://www.kjzz.org/fronteras-desk/2026-05-29/project-blue-data-center-could-use-up-to-31-million-gallons-of-water-per-year)). Families near Meta's Newton County, Georgia data center sued in September 2026, alleging construction fouled their wells; Meta says its own study found no effect ([Atlanta Journal-Constitution](https://www.ajc.com/news/2026/09/georgia-families-sue-meta-accuse-its-data-center-of-tainting-their-water/)).
- **The other side of the ledger:** Google reports the energy per median text prompt fell 33-fold in twelve months to 0.24 watt-hours (company figures, not independently verified) ([Google Cloud, August 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference)). The US expects a record 86 GW of new generating capacity in 2026, 79% solar and batteries and only 6.3 GW gas ([EIA, February 2026](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67205)). The IEA noted in June 2026 that load growth "does not necessarily raise per unit prices" and that its 2030 outlook is unchanged because supply chains are slowing the build-out ([IEA](https://asiacleanenergyforum.adb.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Key-questions-on-energy-and-AI.pdf)). Duke University researchers estimate the existing US grid could absorb about 98 GW of new demand if large users cut use in about 0.5% of hours ([Duke Nicholas Institute](https://nicholasinstitute.duke.edu/articles/ai-and-data-center-power-demand-skyrockets-flexible-load-integration-becomes-critical)).

## Existing coverage
Several rules now apply, mostly new and not yet tested:
- **Texas Senate Bill 6** (signed June 2025) lets the grid operator cut power to new loads of 75 MW or more in emergencies, creates a paid demand-reduction program, and requires disclosure of backup generation ([Bracewell](https://www.bracewell.com/resources/texas-senate-bill-6-ushers-in-major-overhaul-of-large-load-interconnection-and-grid-access-rules/)). Detailed rules are being written through 2026 ([Foley & Lardner](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/03/public-utility-commission-of-texas-issues-proposed-rules-for-large-load-interconn/)).
- **Ohio** regulators approved AEP Ohio's data center tariff in July 2025: new data centers over 25 MW pay for at least 85% of the capacity they reserve for up to 12 years, whether they use it or not ([POWER](https://www.powermag.com/regulator-approves-aep-ohios-landmark-data-center-tariff/)).
- **Virginia** regulators created a separate rate class for customers of 25 MW or more in November 2025, with minimum payment of 85% of contracted transmission and distribution demand and 60% of generation demand, effective January 2027 ([E&E News](https://www.eenews.net/articles/virginia-to-treat-data-centers-differently-on-the-grid/)).
- **Federal:** after a Department of Energy directive in October 2025, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ordered all six regional grid operators in June 2026 to show their large-load rules are adequate or propose reforms, including cost transparency ([Orrick, July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/FERC-Show-Cause-Orders-Signal-Broad-Reform-to-Large-Load-Interconnection-Policies)).
- **European Union:** the Energy Efficiency Directive requires data centers with significant energy use to report energy and water performance to a public EU database; a common rating scheme was proposed in September 2026 ([European Commission](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/energy-efficiency/energy-efficiency-targets-directive-and-rules/energy-efficiency-directive/energy-performance-data-centres_en)).
- Ordinary air, water-permit and local zoning rules apply to gas plants and data centers. The US has no federal requirement for data centers to report energy or water use, and no rule governs whether companies' climate-benefit claims for AI are accurate.

## Triage
1. **Covered by law and enforced?** Partly. Cost-allocation rules exist in a few states and are just taking effect; federal reform is mid-process. Emissions and water outcomes are largely unregulated as such.
2. **An action both sides accept?** **Yes.** Both camps support (a) making large new loads pay the grid costs they cause, as Ohio and Virginia now do, (b) flexible connection, where data centers get connected faster in exchange for cutting use at peak, which optimists see as the cheapest way to serve AI and skeptics see as protection against overbuilding, and (c) mandatory public reporting of data center energy and water use, which Koomey's forecast critique and Luccioni's rebound critique both depend on.
3. *(Skipped, because step 2 applies.)*

**Result: Policy, act now** on cost allocation, flexible connection and reporting. **Prepare and watch** on gas lock-in, tied to signposts 1 and 2.

**Communication note:** Your own AI use is a rounding error; the buildings are what matter. Data centers are a small share of world electricity but a large and fast-growing share in particular places, and that is where bills and water fights show up. Both "AI will boil the oceans" and "efficiency solves it" leave out half the numbers.

## Related scenarios
- Opposite in the same domain: "Faster discovery and cheaper clean energy" (the same build-out, judged by what it produces).
- Combines with: "Concentrated power or AI-enabled surveillance" (who captures the gains and who pays the costs).
- Reduced by: "AI disappoints" (though a sudden pullback could leave customers paying for plants and lines built for demand that never came).
