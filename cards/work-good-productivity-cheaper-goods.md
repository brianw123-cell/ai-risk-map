# Broad productivity gains and cheaper goods

**In one sentence:** AI makes workers across the economy noticeably more productive, and the gains reach ordinary people as higher real wages and lower prices rather than staying with the firms that own the technology.

**Design note:** for a good-direction card, "worried" means worried this won't happen or is oversold, not worried that it will. The scenario has two links that can break separately: productivity has to rise across the economy, and the gains have to reach workers and shoppers. Evidence for the first link is growing. Evidence for the second is close to absent so far. "Productivity" here means output per hour worked.

## Preconditions
- The task-level gains seen in field studies (faster, better work on the tasks AI suits) carry over to whole firms and industries, not just to a few tasks.
- Adoption spreads beyond the technology sector and large firms. About 18% of US firms used AI at the end of 2025 ([Federal Reserve, April 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/monitoring-ai-adoption-in-the-u-s-economy-20260403.html)).
- Competition is strong enough that firms pass cost savings on as lower prices.
- Workers have enough bargaining power, or enough new kinds of work, that higher output per hour shows up in pay.
- The costs of building AI (chips, data centers, electricity) do not push prices up faster than the productivity gains push them down.

## Timing and reversibility
- **Earliest plausible date:** task-level gains are measured now. Economy-wide effects are modeled to build slowly: the Penn Wharton Budget Model puts AI's peak yearly contribution to productivity growth at about 0.2 percentage points in 2032 ([Penn Wharton Budget Model, September 2025](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/p/2025-09-08-the-projected-impact-of-generative-ai-on-future-productivity-growth/)).
- **Speed:** slow-building. Past general-purpose technologies showed a dip in measured productivity while firms reorganized, then a rise, a pattern economists call the "productivity J-curve" ([Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3266241)).
- **Reversibility:** not applicable for a benefit. The relevant risk is that gains arrive but are captured by a few firms, or are offset by higher prices for AI-related goods and power.

## The case that this happens (optimistic)
*Short excerpts for orientation only. A person from this side will choose the final quotes at sign-off.*

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Stanford economist, argued in a February 2026 Financial Times guest article that US productivity growth of about 2.7% in 2025 showed AI gains starting to appear, with the economy "transitioning out of this investment phase into a harvest phase" ([The Decoder, February 2026, secondary](https://the-decoder.com/stanfords-brynjolfsson-sees-ai-boosting-us-productivity-but-he-also-co-founded-an-ai-consulting-firm/)). The same coverage notes he co-founded Workhelix, a firm that helps companies adopt AI.
- David Autor, MIT labor economist, argues that AI could let workers with foundational training do expert work now reserved for elites, narrowing pay gaps, while stressing that "AI will not decide how AI is used" ([Noema, February 2024](https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-could-help-rebuild-the-middle-class/)).

**Plain summary (needs sign-off from this side):** Field studies keep finding real gains. Customer support agents resolved 15% more issues per hour with an AI assistant, and the least experienced workers gained the most. Consultants using GPT-4 finished more tasks, faster, at higher quality. The biggest gains go to less experienced workers, which is the opposite of past technology waves that rewarded the already skilled. US productivity growth has averaged 2.1% a year since late 2019, above the 1.5% of the previous business cycle, and the J-curve pattern says the large gains come after the reorganizing. Cheaper expertise means cheaper services, and cheaper services are how productivity has always raised living standards.

## The case that it doesn't (worried this is oversold)
- Daron Acemoglu, MIT economist and 2024 Nobel laureate, estimates AI will add roughly 0.55% to total factor productivity (output growth not explained by more labor or capital) over a decade, saying of automation that "it's not as easy as sometimes it's presumed" ([Fortune, June 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/21/nobel-laureate-daron-acemoglu-ai-productivity-capitalism-democracy/)).
- Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard, studying 25,000 Danish workers in 11 AI-exposed occupations linked to official pay records, found "a precise null" effect of chatbot adoption on earnings and hours, ruling out average effects larger than 2% ([Brookings, October 2026](https://brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai)).

**Plain summary (needs sign-off from this side):** Task gains are not economy-wide gains. Studies pick well-defined tasks where AI shines, and on tasks outside its abilities AI made consultants worse. When experienced developers were measured instead of asked, they were slower with AI while believing they were faster. Economy-wide, the gains so far look small, wages for AI users have not moved, and the share of output paid to workers is at a record low. Meanwhile AI is pushing up the prices of chips, computers, software and electricity. Robert Gordon of Northwestern University bet Brynjolfsson that US productivity growth will average below 1.8% a year from 2020 to 2030 ([Bloomberg Government, May 2026, secondary](https://news.bgov.com/artificial-intelligence/a-400-ai-bet-thats-a-high-stakes-wager-on-the-future-of-work)). Recent strength in the numbers may reflect data-center spending rather than AI making work more efficient ([The Decoder, February 2026, secondary](https://the-decoder.com/stanfords-brynjolfsson-sees-ai-boosting-us-productivity-but-he-also-co-founded-an-ai-consulting-firm/)).

## Common ground
- AI raises output per hour on many specific tasks, with the largest gains for less experienced workers ([Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044)).
- On some tasks AI makes skilled people worse, and people misjudge their own AI speed-up ([Mollick, September 2023](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged); [METR, July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)).
- No one has yet shown AI lowering consumer prices economy-wide. In 2026, AI-related goods prices are rising ([Governor Cook, September 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260928a.htm)).
- Whether gains reach wages depends on choices by firms and policymakers, not on the technology alone. Autor, Acemoglu and Federal Reserve Governor Cook all say some version of this.

## Cruxes
1. **Does US productivity growth stay above 1.8% a year through 2030, and can AI be shown to be a cause?** This is the Brynjolfsson and Gordon bet. *Check: BLS Productivity and Costs releases, annually through early 2031.*
2. **Do real wages start rising with productivity, or does the labor share keep falling?** Productivity rose 2.2% in the year to mid-2026 while real hourly pay fell 0.1% ([BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf)). *Check: BLS labor share and real compensation, 2027 to 2028.*
3. **Do AI-adopting industries show lower price growth once the data-center building boom slows?** *Check: Federal Reserve and BEA price analyses, 2027 to 2029.*

## Evidence confidence
**Low** for the scenario as a whole. Task-level evidence is solid (randomized and staggered field studies with thousands of workers). Economy-wide productivity evidence is early and disputed, and there is no evidence yet that gains are reaching wages or consumer prices.

## Signposts
1. BLS nonfarm business productivity growth stays above 2% a year for 2026 and 2027 (the current business cycle has averaged 2.1% a year since late 2019, the same as the long-run average since 1947, per [BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf)).
2. The BLS labor share (the part of output paid to workers) rises back above 55%. It was 52.8% in the second quarter of 2026, the lowest since records began in 1947 ([BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf)).
3. Real hourly compensation grows by 1% or more over four quarters. It fell 0.1% in the year to mid-2026 ([BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf)).
4. Census survey AI use passes 35% of US firms (about 21.5% in mid-2026, per [The Productivity Institute](https://lab.productivity.ac.uk/insights/q2-2026-us-productivity-growth-revival-continues/)).
5. A peer-reviewed study finds lower price growth in AI-adopting industries in US data, after controlling for other causes.

## Evidence so far
- **Customer support (staggered rollout, 5,172 agents):** AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. Less experienced agents gained in speed and quality; the most experienced saw small speed gains and small quality declines ([Quarterly Journal of Economics, February 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044)). The 2023 working paper version reported a 34% gain for novices ([NBER, 2023](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)).
- **Boston Consulting Group (randomized, 758 consultants):** on tasks within AI's abilities, consultants with GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with about 40% higher quality. On a task designed to sit outside AI's abilities, accuracy fell from 84% without AI to 60 to 70% with it ([Ethan Mollick, co-author, September 2023](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged)).
- **Experienced open-source developers (randomized, 16 developers, 246 tasks):** AI tools made them 19% slower, while they believed AI had sped them up by 20% ([METR, July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). METR's late-2025 follow-up could not produce a reliable estimate, partly because many developers refused to work without AI; METR believes developers are now sped up more than in early 2025 but says its data is "very weak evidence" of how much ([METR, February 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).
- **Denmark, administrative pay records:** no measurable effect of chatbot adoption on earnings or hours, with effects above 2% ruled out ([Humlum and Vestergaard, Brookings, October 2026](https://brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai)).
- **US productivity statistics:** nonfarm business productivity rose 2.2% in 2025 (annual average) and 2.8% from late 2024 to late 2025 ([BLS, March 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod2_03052026.pdf)). In the year to the second quarter of 2026 it rose 2.2%, real hourly compensation fell 0.1%, and the labor share hit 52.8%, the lowest on record ([BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/prod2.pdf)). An analysis by The Productivity Institute says AI "could be contributing" to the revival, mainly via the tech sector, but does not quantify it ([Martin Fleming, 2026](https://lab.productivity.ac.uk/insights/q2-2026-us-productivity-growth-revival-continues/)).
- **Federal Reserve views:** Minneapolis Fed economists found that three broad productivity measures through early 2026 showed no boom, and that AI-related goods and software prices were rising ([Minneapolis Fed, August 2026](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/how-is-ai-influencing-interest-rates-investment-productivity-prices-and-more)). Governor Lisa Cook said in September 2026 that AI "appears to be adding inflationary pressures" in the short run, expects productivity to bring "modest disinflation" within a few years, and warned that gains may not pass to wages if workers' bargaining power is low ([Federal Reserve, September 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260928a.htm)).
- **Self-reported gains:** US generative AI users reported saving 5.4% of their work hours in late 2024 ([St. Louis Fed, February 2025](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2025/feb/impact-generative-ai-work-productivity)). Of small employer firms using AI, 71% report higher productivity (cited by [Governor Cook, September 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260928a.htm)). Self-reports overstate gains, as the developer study shows.
- **Exposure:** the IMF estimated in 2024 that almost 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, about 60% in advanced economies and 26% in low-income countries, and that in most scenarios AI will likely worsen overall inequality ([IMF, January 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity)).

## Existing coverage
No law blocks AI productivity gains. Competition (antitrust) law is the main existing tool for making cost savings reach prices, and labor law and collective bargaining shape whether they reach wages. Neither was designed with AI in mind. Official statistics agencies (BLS, BEA, Census) already measure productivity, prices, labor share and firm AI adoption; the Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey added AI questions, which the Federal Reserve uses to track adoption ([Federal Reserve, April 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/monitoring-ai-adoption-in-the-u-s-economy-20260403.html)). There is no official measure of how much of productivity growth comes from AI.

## Triage
*(Flipped for a good scenario: what is blocking it, and does removing the block need policy?)*
1. **Is it blocked by law?** No. The blocks are slow adoption outside tech, the reorganizing firms must do first, weak worker bargaining power, and rising prices for AI inputs.
2. **An action both sides accept?** **Yes, on measurement.** Optimists argue official statistics undercount early AI gains (the J-curve), and skeptics argue the gains are being overstated from task studies and self-reports. Both positions rest on better data, and both camps already rely on administrative records (payroll, tax and productivity series) to argue their case. Funding official tracking of AI's effects on productivity, wages and prices by industry is acceptable to both and matches the measurement action on the companion card. Whether to steer AI toward helping workers rather than replacing them (Acemoglu, Autor) is contested and is not part of this result.
3. *(Skipped.)*

**Result: Policy, act now** on measurement of AI's productivity, wage and price effects. **Communicate** to set realistic expectations.

**Communication note:** Tell people the honest shape of it: AI measurably speeds up many specific tasks, especially for newer workers, but there is no clear economy-wide boom yet, wages have not followed, and in 2026 AI is raising some prices rather than lowering them. "Cheaper everything" is a forecast, not a finding.

## Related scenarios
- Opposite in the same domain: "Mass job loss and widening inequality" (the same productivity, with gains flowing to capital).
- Combines with: "Concentrated power or AI-enabled surveillance" (who owns the gains); "AI's energy and resource demand outpaces its gains" (power and chip costs offsetting cheaper goods); "Personal tutors, care and freed time" (gains taken as time rather than money).
- Reduced by: "AI disappoints."
