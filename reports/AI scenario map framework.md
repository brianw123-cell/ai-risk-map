# Build the AI map both camps sign

**Nobody has built what Emad Mostaque described, and every piece needed to build it already exists somewhere.** The closest prior art is the MIT AI Risk Repository (1,725 coded risks, but no good outcomes, no probabilities and no debate), the International AI Safety Report (a neutral expert assessment that deliberately gives no probabilities and no policy recommendations), the Forecasting Research Institute's tournaments (numbers from both camps, but on only a handful of questions), and a November 2025 joint essay by the authors of *AI 2027* and *AI as Normal Technology* that maps where two opposing worldviews agree. No project puts each scenario, good or bad, on one card with each side's case in its own words, the published probabilities, and a decision about whether it needs policy or public communication. Mostaque's claim that the list of things that could go wrong is "finite" holds only at the top level: the major frameworks agree on about four mechanisms of harm, but the specific risks number in the thousands, so the map should be **finite at the top and open at the bottom**. The most important research finding for the design is that structured debate does not make the two camps converge on numbers, yet they do agree on more policies than their numbers would suggest. That means the map should aim to make disagreement easy to read rather than resolve it, and should triage scenarios by which actions hold up under both camps' estimates. My recommended framework is about 18 scenarios in eight paired domains, a 17-field entry template, probability shown as a spread with its source and date, three decision lenses shown side by side, a four-step triage rule, and paired editors with sign-off from each camp. The first public version is a static Next.js site that Brian can build himself. The real risks are paying for maintenance and being seen as a front for one camp, mostly through who funds it.

## Mostaque asked for a map that sits in an empty spot

Mostaque's proposal came at the end of a discussion about AI regulation on the Moonshots podcast. His core claim was that "there's only, I think, a really finite amount of things that could go wrong, and many of them are already caught by regulation," and that for the rest "we should have an actual mapping project" ([Moonshots, 7 Oct 2026, 48:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LRdb8UmPnh0&t=2907s)). He said that today "there is nowhere that you can go and actually see a proper map" of what the scared and the positive "actually say about each scenario," that it should be "in their own words," and that the point is to see "what requires a policy and regulatory response, and what do we need to communicate to the people" ([Moonshots](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LRdb8UmPnh0&t=2907s)). The quotes come from Brian's cleaned transcript of the YouTube auto-captions, and attribution of this passage to Mostaque is certain because Peter Diamandis addresses him by name just before it.

His description of the empty spot is accurate. **The MIT AI Risk Repository** is the largest open catalog: its May 2026 version holds "74 frameworks containing 1,725 distinct risks," coded by cause (human or AI, intentional or not, before or after deployment) and sorted into seven domains, under a CC BY 4.0 license, meaning anyone may reuse it with credit ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12622)). Its unit is a risk statement pulled from someone else's paper, not a future scenario, and it has no beneficial outcomes, no probabilities and no paired views. Its own launch found that the most thorough single framework missed about 30% of the risks found across all of them ([MIT FutureTech](https://futuretech.mit.edu/news/presenting-the-ai-risk-repository)), which is a warning about any one team's list, this one included. The **AI Incident Database** and the **OECD AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor** record harms that have already happened. The OECD also tracks "hazards," events that "could plausibly lead to an AI incident," and uses a severity ladder that runs from incident to serious incident to "AI disaster" ([OECD AIM methodology](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents-methodology); [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/defining-ai-incidents-and-hazards)). These are evidence feeds for the map, not competitors. The incident database already tags its entries with MIT's codes ([AIID](https://incidentdatabase.ai/taxonomies/mit)), so a map keyed to MIT codes gets incident links almost free.

**The International AI Safety Report** (2026 edition, over 100 experts, an advisory panel nominated by more than 30 countries) is the nearest thing to a climate-style assessment for AI. It sorts risks into misuse, malfunctions and systemic risks, and it openly says views on loss of control "vary widely." It also states plainly that it "does not recommend any policies" and gives no probability estimates ([Extended Summary 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers)). That is the clearest description of the gap: **the map would make exactly the two judgments, probabilities and policy triage, that the leading official assessment chose not to make.** That is legitimate, but it puts the burden on the map to show whose numbers and whose triage it is using.

On the scenario side, Max Tegmark's 12 "aftermath" scenarios run from utopias to extinction but describe end states after superintelligence, and Tegmark says they are not exhaustive ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/ai/ai-aftermath-scenarios/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_3.0)). The UK Government Office for Science built five scenarios for 2030 from five critical uncertainties but **deliberately excluded largely benign futures** ([GOV.UK Annex A](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)). *AI 2027* is one detailed pathway with a single fork ([ai-2027.com](https://ai-2027.com/)). The OECD Expert Group on AI Futures published ten priority benefits alongside ten priority risks, the closest official paired list ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-publications/futures)).

The two pieces of prior art closest to Mostaque's "both sides" format each cover only one or two questions. The Forecasting Research Institute's Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament (XPT) in 2022 had 80 domain experts and 89 superforecasters (people with top track records in forecasting competitions) forecast, debate and update ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/xpt)). The *AI 2027* and *AI as Normal Technology* authors' joint essay laid out what they agree on, where they split, and shared policy priorities such as transparency, independent auditing, and preventing unmonitored self-improvement of AI systems ([Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/common-ground-between-ai-2027-and)). That essay is the best existing template for a single scenario card.

## Mostaque's list is finite at the top and open at the bottom

Whether a "finite" list is defensible depends on the level you count at. At the level of **mechanisms**, the field has converged. The International AI Safety Report uses three buckets (malicious use, malfunctions, systemic) ([IASR 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary)). Google DeepMind uses four (misuse, misalignment, mistakes, structural) ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/taking-a-responsible-path-to-agi/)). The Center for AI Safety uses four (malicious use, AI race, organizational risks, rogue AIs) ([arXiv 2306.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12001v1)). At the level of **specific risks**, the count is 1,700 and rising. Two kinds of risk resist any neat list. Atoosa Kasirzadeh separates "decisive" risks (a sudden takeover) from "accumulative" risks, where many small harms wear down resilience until one trigger causes collapse ([arXiv 2401.07836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07836v3)). Critch and Russell flag harms from "unanticipated interactions of many AI systems" that belong to no single actor, and note that "many exhaustive taxonomies are possible" ([arXiv 2306.06924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.06924)).

Foresight practitioners have three ways to keep a scenario set finite. The **2x2 matrix**, made famous by Shell, crosses two big uncertainties into four quadrants. It is easy for the public to read, but critics document "a systematic omission of potentially critical scenarios" because it reduces the future to two factors ([Roth et al.](https://derroth.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/00-roth-et-al.-n-factorial-scenarios.pdf)). **Morphological analysis** lists the key factors and their possible values, generates every combination, then removes the pairs that cannot coexist, leaving only coherent futures ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_analysis_(problem-solving))). **Cross-impact balance** does the same thing more rigorously: experts rate how much each state of one factor pushes or blocks each state of the others, and free software (ScenarioWizard, from the University of Stuttgart) keeps only the self-consistent combinations ([ZIRIUS](https://www.zirius.uni-stuttgart.de/en/cib-lab/)). The UK's 2023 frontier AI scenarios combined expert workshops with morphological analysis ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)). The climate field's Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) show how to combine the rigorous and the readable: a systematic method underneath, then five named storylines on two plain-language axes, chosen "to span the range of plausible futures," including good ones ([Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-how-shared-socioeconomic-pathways-explore-future-climate-change/)).

The research supports three conclusions. First, a list that is "mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive" (MECE, the consultant's term for no overlaps and no gaps) can only be MECE **relative to the factors you chose**, not relative to reality, and the map should say so on its face. Second, sources organize along different axes (by actor, by speed, by cause), so the map has to pick one axis for the top level and treat the rest as tags, or the categories will overlap. Third, existing scenario sets mix **end states** (Tegmark: where we end up) with **pathways** (*AI 2027*: how we get there) and **near-term conditions** (the UK scenarios: what 2030 looks like). Mixing those in one list breaks mutual exclusivity. The UK set also contributes one scenario most lists forget: "AI disappoints," where progress is modest and investment falls ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)).

Mostaque's second claim, that many harms are "already caught by regulation," is testable scenario by scenario rather than something to assume. The 2026 safety report notes that sophisticated attackers can often bypass current technical safeguards ([Second Key Update](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/second-key-update-technical-safeguards-and-risk-management)), so "illegal" does not mean "prevented." MIT's governance mapping, which in April 2026 classified over 1,000 policy documents from Georgetown's AGORA archive, is a ready source for checking which scenarios existing rules already cover ([airisk.mit.edu](https://airisk.mit.edu/)).

## Fair quotes and honest numbers need separate rules

### Quoting each side in its own words

The best tested rules for representing a dispute fairly come from Wikipedia's neutral point of view policy. It says to weight views "in proportion to the prominence of each viewpoint" in reliable sources, to attribute every opinion to a named source rather than stating it in the encyclopedia's own voice, and to "describe disputes, but not engage in them." It also warns that undue weight can come from placement, length and imagery, not just from what gets included ([Wikipedia NPOV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view)). The International AI Safety Report adds a layered structure: say where there is consensus, "acknowledge where and why there is disagreement," and note dissenting views where differences persist ([UK Gov principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/international-scientific-report-on-advanced-ai-safety-expert-advisory-panel-and-principles-and-procedures/international-scientific-report-on-advanced-ai-safety-principles-and-procedures)).

Three fairness checks have real track records. **Own-camp sign-off** is how Daniel Kahneman's adversarial collaborations worked: opponents agree on the framing and the tests in advance, with a neutral arbiter ([Psychological Science, 2001](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.00350/)). The **Ideological Turing Test** asks whether you can state the other side's view so well that its own holders cannot tell you from a real believer ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/ideological-turing-tests)). It now has a measurable version: a 2025 study of 200 UK participants scored people as passing when opponents rated their version of the other side's argument as highly as arguments written by real believers ([Brand et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12519043/)). **Blind review** is AllSides' strongest method for rating media bias: readers rate content with the source names removed ([AllSides](https://www.allsides.com/media-bias/media-bias-rating-methods)).

The format matters too. Kialo's endlessly branching pro and con trees help beginners find their way around but carry "not much signal" in their crowd votes on argument strength, by one informed user's account ([EA Forum](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/HmYfoKW6FuyFHmwcJ/why-is-argument-mapping-not-more-common-in-ea-rationality/answer/9FdPRr45LNtMWJipX)). ProCon.org's fixed two-column layout, compiled by staff with sources, is rated "least biased" by outside reviewers ([Media Bias/Fact Check](https://mediabiasfactcheck.com/procon-org/)), though ProCon admits that even putting pros on the left can be read as a political signal ([ProCon FAQ](https://www.procon.org/faqs/)). Live debates reward performance: at the 2023 Munk Debate on AI risk, one commentator sympathetic to the losing side felt the weaker arguments "came across quite convincing," and the vote-counting system reportedly failed ([GreaterWrong](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/CA7iLZHNT5xbLK59Y)). The finding is that **a short, fixed template with named, dated sources works better for a lay audience than an open tree or a scored debate.**

The biggest trap is false balance. A study of US newspaper coverage of global warming from 1988 to 2002 found the norm of giving both sides equal space "leads to biased coverage" by making settled science look contested ([Boykoff and Boykoff 2004](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_94928)). AI is different in an important way: on the largest questions there is no consensus to protect, since median estimates for AI-caused existential catastrophe by 2100 ran from 0.12% to 20% between camps in one study ([FRI](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)). So two-sided presentation is appropriate for most scenarios. The risk runs the other way: giving a scenario that only a few people take seriously the same visual weight as one most experts discuss.

### Showing probability without false precision

The probability evidence teaches four things. First, **the gap between camps is large, stable, and not a matter of intelligence.** In the 2022 XPT, domain experts gave a median 3% chance of AI-caused human extinction by 2100 and superforecasters gave 0.38% ([Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-extinction-tournament)). Four months of incentivized debate produced "minimal convergence of beliefs" ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/xpt)). A 2023 follow-up built specifically to make the camps engage ("adversarial collaboration") found that "neither the concerned nor the skeptics substantially updated," and the best short-term crux question each side named was expected to close only a sliver of the gap ([FRI via EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)). A crux is the question whose answer would change your mind. Yet on a 1,000-year horizon the same groups gave 30% and 40% for severe negative effects, so **much of the fight is about speed, not about whether danger exists** ([same](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)). A 2025 accuracy check found both camps underestimated AI progress and the superforecasters were more wrong: experts gave 8.6% to an AI gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad by 2025, superforecasters 2.3%, and it happened in July 2025 ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy)). The worried side was more accurate on near-term AI questions while the skeptic side has better general track records. A map should show both facts and declare no winner.

Second, **wording moves the numbers.** The 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers gave a median 5% to extremely bad outcomes such as extinction, but asking about "human inability to control" AI instead raised the median to 10% ([Grace et al.](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)). The same survey put "high-level machine intelligence" at 50% by 2047 but "full automation of labor" at 50% by 2116, which the authors call a framing effect ([same](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)). The median and the mean also diverge sharply (5% against 9% to 19% depending on wording) because a minority gives very high numbers.

Third, **the "two tribes" picture is too simple.** In that survey, 68.3% thought good outcomes more likely than bad, yet 48% of those optimists still gave at least 5% to extremely bad outcomes, and 59% of pessimists gave at least 5% to extremely good ones ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)). The Forecasting Research Institute's ongoing expert panel, which collects monthly forecasts from 339 experts, finds that uncertainty within each person and disagreement between people each explain about half the total variation ([LEAP](https://etcjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8fc30-the-longitudinal-expert-ai-panel.pdf)).

Fourth, **some numbers can never be checked.** Forecasting platforms like Metaculus have a measurable but modest record on AI questions: a Brier score (a standard accuracy measure where lower is better) of 0.207 against 0.25 for always guessing 50% ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/e9htD7txe8RDdcehm/exploring-metaculus-s-ai-track-record)). Nobody can score an extinction forecast, and Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that scoring rules are "insensitive to overestimating tail risks" ([Normal Technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)).

The best tested way to communicate probability comes from the climate field. The IPCC uses two separate scales: a **likelihood** word tied to a number range ("likely" means 66 to 100%, "unlikely" 0 to 33%) used only where evidence allows, and a separate **confidence** rating from very low to very high based on the quality and agreement of the evidence ([IPCC guidance](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/ipcc_uncertainty-guidance-note.pdf)). A study across 24 countries found readers pull probability words toward 50%, and printing the number range next to the word improved correct readings ([Budescu et al. 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcli/v4y2014i6d10.1038_nclimate2194.html)), though more than half of readings still fell outside the intended range in earlier work ([UCL review](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1528764/3/Harris_R1%20-%20FINAL.pdf)). The lesson for an AI map is that **the spread of opinion is the story**, and a single headline number would hide it.

## Decision lenses should sit side by side, not be chosen for the reader

Mostaque framed the problem as a choice between two decision methods. "Normally we do an expected utility calculation," he said, but "when we deal with uncertainty, we do minimax regret," and "the fear stories lead us to minimize for maximum regret" ([Moonshots](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LRdb8UmPnh0&t=2907s)). **Expected utility** means multiplying each outcome's value by its probability and picking the option with the best average. **Minimax regret** (from Leonard Savage, 1951) means picking the option whose worst-case shortfall, compared with the best choice you could have made in each scenario, is smallest. It needs no probabilities ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/savage-minimax-regret/)).

One technical point is worth knowing. What Mostaque describes ("don't have a nuclear power station, don't try and build a superintelligence") is closer to the **precautionary principle**, which puts avoiding catastrophe first even at substantial cost to benefits ([Philosophy of Science](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C5F5919A78917692661D5F74EC072DB9/S0031824824000400a.pdf/deep-uncertainty-and-incommensurability-general-cautions-about-precaution.pdf)). Regret scoring was actually designed to avoid being taken over by a single disaster in which every option fails, by focusing on scenarios where the choice makes a difference ([arXiv 2203.01420](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.01420)). His broader point still stands: methods that ignore probabilities give vivid worst cases heavy influence. Minimax regret is also "maximally ambiguity averse," meaning it can give up large benefits to avoid uncertainty ([EA Forum](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/kCBQHWqbk4Nrns8P7/model-based-policy-analysis-under-deep-uncertainty)), and it can be gamed by adding irrelevant options ([arXiv 2403.02960](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02960)).

A third approach fits this project better than either. RAND defines **deep uncertainty** as the situation where parties "do not know, or agree on" the model connecting actions to consequences or the likelihood of future events. Its **Robust Decision Making** method tests candidate policies across hundreds or thousands of plausible futures, using computation "not to make better predictions, but to yield better decisions" ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9701.html); [RAND, Lempert](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP67834.html)). Narayanan and Kapoor make the same point in plain terms: adopt policies "that help across a range of risk estimates" ([Normal Technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)). I found no study applying any of these methods directly to AI scenarios.

The key insight is that the three methods need different inputs. Expected utility needs agreed probabilities, which the evidence above shows do not exist. Minimax regret needs a list of scenarios and their outcomes. Robust Decision Making needs scenarios plus candidate policies. One methods guide notes that a regret table "breaks at step one" if you cannot list the futures ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/minimax-regret-rule/)). **A finite scenario map is exactly the input both probability-free methods require**, which makes the project useful to readers on both sides of Mostaque's argument. The supporting fact for triage is that the camps agree on policy more than on numbers: the *AI 2027* and *Normal Technology* authors share several concrete policy priorities despite very different forecasts ([Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/common-ground-between-ai-2027-and)). So the map can sort scenarios into policy and communication without first settling the probabilities.

## Benefits lack a shared taxonomy, so the map has to build one

The positive side is much less organized than the risk side. **I found no peer-reviewed, general taxonomy of AI benefits comparable to the MIT risk repository**, and that absence is itself a finding. The most rigorous study is Vinuesa et al. (2020), whose expert panel found AI could help achieve 134 of the 169 UN Sustainable Development Goal targets and hinder 59 ([Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-14108-y)). The numbers overlap, so some targets were scored as both helped and hurt, which supports pairing every benefit with its matching downside. Dario Amodei's "Machines of Loving Grace" sets out five domains: biology and health, neuroscience and mental health, economic development and poverty, peace and governance, and work and meaning ([darioamodei.com](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)). The OECD expert group's ten benefits include accelerated science, productivity gains, and "better sense-making and forecasting" ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-publications/futures)). Diamandis and Kotler's *Abundance* covers water, food, energy, healthcare, education and freedom ([Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.co.uk/books/Abundance/Peter-H-Diamandis/Exponential-Technology-Series/9781451616835)). Google DeepMind describes mechanisms of scientific benefit such as knowledge synthesis and simulation ([ibl.ai summary](https://ibl.ai/blog/google-deepmind-a-new-golden-age-of-discovery)). The Foresight Institute's Existential Hope project explicitly offers "a counter-narrative" to catastrophe ([Existential Hope](https://theexistentialhope.substack.com/about)), and the Future of Life Institute ran a $100,000 worldbuilding contest for "plausible and aspirational" AI futures ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/project/worldbuilding-competition/)).

Across these sources about eight benefit categories recur: health and lifespan, mental health, science and discovery, economic growth and poverty reduction, energy and environment, education, governance and decision-making, and work and meaning. Two structural differences matter for design. Benefit lists are organized by **domain** (health, energy) while risk lists are organized by **mechanism** (misuse, accident, loss of control). And benefit writing mostly describes end states, not the paths to them or the early signs that they are arriving, so positive entries will start thinner than risk entries. The most practical way to put good and bad on one grid is to organize by domain and give each domain a good-direction and a bad-direction scenario, with mechanism as a tag. This follows the paired approach of both Vinuesa and the OECD. The Center for AI Safety's overview already includes an "ideal scenario" for each risk category ([arXiv 2306.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12001v1)), which shows the pairing works on the risk side too.

## Capture comes through money and moderation more than argument

The research on governance points to one conclusion: **no single safeguard is enough**, and the most likely ways a map like this gets captured are who pays for it and who decides what gets removed or merged.

The IPCC has the strongest review model. Anyone with relevant qualifications can register as a reviewer, every comment gets a written response from the authors, and after publication the drafts, comments and responses are all released, with reviewers credited by name ([IPCC fact sheet](https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/2024/04/IPCCFactSheet_ReviewProcess.pdf)). Its line-by-line government approval of the summary needs a treaty body and does not transfer to a personal project.

**Bridging systems** are the strongest known defense against one camp taking over a rating process. Polis, used by Taiwan's vTaiwan process, has people vote agree, disagree or pass on short statements, groups them into opinion clusters, and highlights statements every cluster supports. It has no reply button, which Audrey Tang argues leaves "no room for troll to grow" ([Computational Democracy Project](https://compdemocracy.org/Polis/); [Tang](https://archive.tw/speech/433959)). Community Notes on X shows a note only when people who usually disagree both rate it helpful ([Wojcik et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.15723)). Both have known weaknesses. A 2025 simulation found that a coordinated 5 to 20% of bad raters can suppress targeted helpful notes, and that over 40% of genuinely helpful notes never get published ([Truong et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02615)). Bridging also filters toward bland statements: when Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project used Polis with about 1,000 representative US adults, both "prioritize the collective good" and its opposite were dropped because neither won support from both opinion groups. The organizers also admit some moderation removals were subjective ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/collective-constitutional-ai-aligning-a-language-model-with-public-input)). The lesson is to use bridging with recruited panels rather than open signup, to publish what was removed and why, and to keep a visible section for what did not bridge.

Funding is where perception of capture forms. The large, fast funders who give to individuals are mostly safety-focused and linked to effective altruism, a movement the optimistic camp distrusts. Reporting in Politico and Semafor criticized Open Philanthropy for funding AI policy staff at RAND, Georgetown's CSET and others ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/10/20/2023/the-new-think-tanks-influencing-ai-policy-in-washington)). The best precedent for how a balanced project uses forecasts, the 2023 adversarial collaboration, was funded by Open Philanthropy, and some of its "concerned" participants were referred by Open Philanthropy staff ([FRI via EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)). The optimistic camp's prominent voices dismiss catastrophe scenarios outright (Yann LeCun: "These doomsday scenarios are absurd") ([Fox News](https://foxnews.com/politics/facebook-cofounder-dustin-moskovitz-effective-altruism-network-tied-ai-regulation-push)), so the map will draw attack from both directions. The Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index picked an all-academic review panel to reduce conflicts of interest ([FLI](https://futureoflife.org/document/fli-ai-safety-index-2024/)), but an independent panel is not the same as a panel balanced across camps. I found no AI project that has recruited advisors explicitly from both the safety and the acceleration camps, so doing so would be new. The Collective Intelligence Project refuses commercial money entirely ([CIP](https://cip.org/funding-partnerships)), while the AI Incident Database's corporate sponsors get "direct input" ([AIID](https://incidentdatabase.ai/blog/funding-the-aiid-part-1/)), a model this map should avoid.

## Small launches worked, and maintenance is what fails

Every comparable project started small. The AI Incident Database launched in November 2020 with **77 incidents** and a public submission form with an editor reviewing each entry ([The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/cataloging-ai-gone-wrong)). By 2025 it had 400,000+ active users, and it now says philanthropic grants are poorly suited to paying for ongoing maintenance ([AIID](https://incidentdatabase.ai/blog/funding-the-aiid-part-1/)). The MIT repository launched with 700+ risks from a literature review and grew through versioned updates ([MIT FutureTech](https://futuretech.mit.edu/news/presenting-the-ai-risk-repository)). *AI 2027* was one scenario website built by about five people over "more than half a year" ([AI Futures blog](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/our-first-project-ai-2027)). Epoch AI began as a volunteer effort around a single paper ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/blog/epoch-impact-report-2022)). The cautionary example is Stampy's AI Safety Info, built by volunteers, which went on a "skeleton crew" budget of about $6,000 a month, half its normal rate, after a funding application failed ([Every.org](https://www.every.org/aisafetyinfo/f/keep-stampy-alive)).

Money for this kind of work comes from two pools. The **safety-focused pool** funds individuals quickly: the Long-Term Future Fund averaged about $34,600 per grant over one year ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/nLxpFeEs6kAdgjRWz)), the Survival and Flourishing Fund gave $31,000 to a one-person watchdog early on ([Manifund](https://manifund.org/projects/the-midas-project)), and Manifund, a public grant platform, handles payouts to individuals and publishes the reasoning ([Manifund](https://dev.manifund.org/about/regranting)). The **civic pool** is larger but slower and favors established nonprofits. Humanity AI, a $500 million five-year coalition co-chaired by MacArthur and Omidyar Network, launched in October 2025 with democracy, education and labor among its priorities ([Mellon Foundation](https://www.mellon.org/news/humanity-ai-commits-500m-build-people-centered-future-ai)). Its stated aim of curbing AI developers' influence places it on one side of some debates ([AP via ADN](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2025/10/14/foundations-want-to-curb-ai-developers-influence-with-500-million-aimed-at-centering-human-needs/)). OpenAI's People-First AI Fund gave $40.5 million to 208 US nonprofits but requires 501(c)(3) status ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/my-MM/index/people-first-ai-fund-grantees/)). A 501(c)(3) is a US tax-exempt charity, and a "fiscal sponsor" is an existing charity that accepts grants on a project's behalf. Money from AI companies would be a credibility problem for a map that assesses scenarios involving those companies.

For partners, a **data partnership** is more realistic than a host institution in year one: link to live Metaculus questions, cite the XPT and the AI researcher survey for probabilities, and tag scenarios with MIT codes, all without anyone's approval. A university partner to review the taxonomy, as Georgetown's CSET did for the AI Incident Database a year after its launch ([AIID](https://incidentdatabase.ai/blog/the-first-taxonomy-of-ai-incidents)), is a realistic second-year goal.

## Recommended framework: eighteen scenarios, one card each

*Everything in this section is my design recommendation, built from the findings above. It is not a finding.*

**Top-level structure.** I recommend two layers. The **big-picture layer** is a two-axis view in the style of the climate SSPs, built on the two cruxes the research identifies: how fast capability grows and spreads (gradual "normal technology" against fast transformation) and whether control and benefits stay broadly shared (shared and overseeable against concentrated or lost). That gives four named quadrants. They are where readers start, not the scenarios themselves. The **scenario layer** sits underneath: eight domains, each with a good-direction and a bad-direction scenario, plus two scenarios that fit no domain. That makes 18, inside the 12-to-20 cap the governance research suggests. Each scenario carries tags for mechanism (misuse, malfunction, loss of control, systemic, matching the International AI Safety Report's buckets), MIT domain code, and which big-picture quadrants it fits in. Behind the scenes, I recommend building the list with a light morphological pass (four to six driving factors, two to four values each, impossible pairs removed) and publishing that grid, so readers can see what the 18 cover and what they leave out.

| Domain | Good-direction scenario | Bad-direction scenario | Main mechanism tags |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health and biology | Medical progress compressed into years | AI-assisted biological or chemical attack | Misuse |
| Work and income | Broad productivity gains and cheaper goods | Mass job loss and widening inequality | Systemic |
| Science, energy and environment | Faster discovery and cheaper clean energy | AI's energy and resource demand outpaces gains | Systemic |
| Information and democracy | Better shared understanding and forecasting | Manipulation, fraud and collapse of trust | Misuse, systemic |
| Security and infrastructure | Defenders gain the advantage | Cheap scaled cyberattacks and critical-system failures | Misuse, malfunction |
| Power and governance | Wider access and more accountable institutions | Concentrated power or an AI surveillance state | Systemic, misuse |
| Daily life, mind and meaning | Personal tutors, care and freed time | Over-reliance, lost skills, unhealthy AI companions | Systemic |
| Control of advanced AI | Powerful AI that stays aligned and overseeable | Loss of control over advanced AI | Loss of control |
| No domain | AI disappoints: modest progress, investment falls | Unknown, combined and slow-building risks | All |

The last row matters. "AI disappoints" keeps the neutral outcome visible, and the open "unknown, combined and slow-building" entry is how the map stays honest about Kasirzadeh's accumulative risks and Critch and Russell's interaction effects. New scenarios get logged there first and promoted to the main list only if they pass the inclusion test below.

**Fields for each scenario entry.** I recommend the following template, which combines the Center for AI Safety's structure (hazards, stories, ideal scenario, mitigations), the UK scenarios' driving factors, foresight signposts, and the joint essay's common-ground format.

| Field | What it holds | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| Name and summary | Neutral name agreed by both camps, one sentence | Names carry framing |
| Layer and direction | Pathway or end state; good, bad, mixed or neutral | Keeps categories from overlapping |
| Tags | Mechanism, MIT domain code, big-picture quadrant | Links to MIT, incident and governance data |
| Preconditions | Values of the driving factors this scenario needs | Shows what would have to be true |
| Timing and reversibility | Earliest plausible date, sudden or slow-building, can it be undone, OECD severity level | Severity and irreversibility drive triage |
| Worried case | Two or three dated quotes from primary sources, plus a short plain summary signed off by that camp | Mostaque's "in their own words" |
| Optimistic case | Same format, signed off by that camp | Same |
| Common ground | Statements both camps endorse | The joint-essay template |
| Cruxes | Two or three questions that would move each side, with resolution dates | Turns argument into something to watch |
| Probability estimates | Each estimate with its source, group, exact wording, date, spread, and whether it can ever be scored | The spread is the story |
| Evidence confidence | IPCC-style very low to very high | Separates "how likely" from "how solid" |
| Signposts | Three to five observable early indicators with thresholds | Shows when a scenario is starting |
| Evidence so far | Linked incidents and hazards; measured benefits for good scenarios | Shows what has already started |
| Existing coverage | Laws and rules that already apply, and whether enforcement works | Tests Mostaque's "already regulated" claim |
| Triage result | Category, reasoning, and a communication note | The output Mostaque asked for |
| Related scenarios | What it leads to or combines with | Handles interactions between scenarios |
| Version record | Last reviewed, who signed off, changelog | Accountability over time |

**Probability and disagreement display rules.** I recommend that the map never publish a single number of its own for any scenario. Every number is attributed to a named source (superforecasters, a researcher survey, a forecasting platform, a scenario's authors) and shown with the exact question wording, the date, and the spread, at minimum the middle half of answers, or every answer as dots where the data allows. Where median and mean diverge sharply, show both. Never average numbers from different questions, because the wording studies show they are not the same question. Pair any probability word with its IPCC number range, and show a separate evidence-confidence badge, which for most catastrophe numbers will honestly read "low: cannot be scored." Flag any number older than about 18 months, and note that all XPT figures predate ChatGPT. Where no estimate exists, say "no published estimate" rather than filling the space. For disagreement, use a "weight of opinion" bar based on surveys and forecast aggregates instead of equal columns, so that a fringe scenario looks like one. Alternate or randomize which camp appears first. Show a "did not bridge" section beside the common-ground section. Show the three decision lenses side by side for each candidate policy: how it does using median estimates (expected utility), its worst-case regret across all 18 scenarios (minimax regret), and in how many scenarios it does acceptably (robust decision-making). This lets readers watch Mostaque's effect happen, a vivid low-probability catastrophe dominating one lens and not the others, rather than the map choosing a lens for them.

**The triage rule.** Mostaque's two buckets need one change: almost every scenario needs public communication, so I recommend that **every card gets a communication note, and the triage decides whether it also gets a policy flag and which kind.** The rule runs in four steps, in order. Step one is a coverage check: is the harmful path already illegal or regulated? If it is and enforcement works, the card is "Covered: communicate." If rules exist but enforcement is weak, it gets an "enforcement problem" policy flag. Step two is a robust-action test: is there an action that both camps' signed-off statements accept, or that does acceptably across the whole scenario set? If so, the card is "Policy: act now," because the action does not depend on settling the probability fight. Step three is a stakes test: is the scenario severe and hard to reverse, with at least one credible published estimate placing it within about ten years, but with no agreed action? If so, the card is "Policy: prepare and watch," meaning develop options now and tie them to specific crux results and signposts that would trigger action. This is the one step where worst-case reasoning legitimately leads, and limiting it to this step answers Mostaque's worry about fear stories without dismissing the worried camp. Step four covers everything else: "Communicate," with the purpose named (correct a misconception, set realistic expectations, or help people prepare for or benefit from a change). Good-direction scenarios go through the same steps, with the question flipped to "what is blocking this, and does removing the block need policy?" Publish the reasoning for each step on the card, so anyone can disagree with a specific step rather than with the result as a whole.

**Inclusion test for new scenarios.** A scenario joins the main list only if a named, credible proponent has written it up publicly, it is meaningfully different from existing entries, and a balanced panel agrees it deserves a slot. Variants sit underneath an existing entry. This follows the governance notes' advice for preventing the list from growing without limit, and it replaces Mostaque's informal move of "putting aside the extreme stories" with a stated rule.

**Governance.** I recommend defining "worried" and "optimistic" **per scenario rather than as two fixed tribes**, since the survey data shows most people hold mixed views and someone can be relaxed about loss of control yet worried about concentrated power. For each scenario, at least two named people from each side sign off that their side's case is fairly stated, with a right to swap in a quote they prefer. Editorial roles are held in pairs, one from each camp, with a neutral process chair who rules on procedure but has no vote on content. Each release goes through a short public review window in the style of the IPCC: every comment gets a written answer, and all comments and answers are published. Store the whole map as structured files in a public Git repository under CC BY 4.0, which is compatible with MIT's license, so anyone can fork it if they believe it has been captured. The possibility of a fork is itself a check on capture. Any public rating (bridging votes, blind fairness checks) uses recruited, balanced panels rather than open signup. Publish every removed or merged statement with the reason. Keep a public funding page listing every funder above a set amount, cap any single funder's share (one third is a reasonable starting cap), aim for at least one funder from each pool, and take no money from AI companies. One more point: Mostaque and the Moonshots panel lean optimistic, so the project's origin will be read as one camp's idea. Credit him openly and say so on the methodology page.

**Phased plan.** I recommend the following phases, each with a clear test to pass before moving to the next.

| Phase | What ships | Money | Test to pass before moving on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Private prototype | Field template as a data schema; four fully drafted cards (loss of control, work and income, AI disappoints, health) | Self-funded | A worried reader and an optimistic reader each say their side's case is fair |
| 1. Public first version (v1.0) | All 18 cards, the big-picture view, methodology page, funding page, correction form with editor review | Self-funded | Live, with corrections coming in and answered |
| 2. Sign-off and review | Four to six paired advisors; own-camp sign-off on every card; first IPCC-style review cycle; quarterly release v1.1 | First small grant (Manifund, Long-Term Future Fund or Survival and Flourishing Fund, tens of thousands of dollars) plus an approach to the civic pool | Every card carries sign-off from both camps |
| 3. Measured fairness | Blind fairness checks with published pass rates; Polis rounds with a recruited panel for common ground; decision-lens comparison; a university partner reviewing the taxonomy | Fiscal sponsor so civic funders can give | Published fairness scores for every card |
| 4. Living map | Live forecasting links; a crux tracker that updates as questions resolve; coverage checks linked to governance datasets | Ongoing maintenance funding from at least two pools | Runs on a few hours a month of upkeep |

On the build, I recommend keeping the content as Markdown or YAML files in Git as the single source of truth, because that gives line-by-line change history and makes forking possible, and using Supabase only for corrections, submissions and later panel votes. A static Next.js site over those files fits Brian's stack and keeps hosting costs low, which matters because the AI Incident Database's hosting costs spiked once traffic outgrew free tiers. The website is the quick part. The slow parts are sourcing dated primary quotes, recruiting people from both camps to sign off, and keeping the cards current. Those decide whether the project survives.

## Conclusion

The research changes how Mostaque's idea should be pitched. His proposal reads as a way to show that fears are overblown, but the evidence suggests its real value is different: it is the missing input that *both* decision styles need. Optimists get a place to show that most harms are already covered and that rare catastrophes should not drive every choice. The worried camp gets the finite scenario list that minimax regret and robust decision-making require before they can be used at all. And because the camps agree on policies more readily than on probabilities, a triage built on robust actions can produce useful output even while the forecasting fight continues indefinitely, which the tournament evidence says it will.

The hardest problem is social, not technical: getting two named people from each camp to sign off on 18 cards, and funding the upkeep from more than one pool. A useful early test, before any public launch, is whether the four prototype cards can get sign-off from one worried and one optimistic reader. If that fails on the loss-of-control card, the format needs rethinking. If it succeeds, the rest is mainly sourcing and persistence, which is the kind of steady, maintained curation that made the incident database and the MIT repository credible.
