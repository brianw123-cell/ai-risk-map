# Benefits taxonomies and methods for a finite AI scenario map

Research notes for the "AI scenario map" framework designer. Current as of October 2026. Every factual claim has a link. Where I only reached a secondary summary, I say so.

## 1. What exists on the positive side? (benefit taxonomies and positive-scenario work)

### Takeaway
Benefit taxonomies exist, but none is as mature as the risk taxonomies. The same handful of benefit categories recurs across very different sources: health and longevity, science and discovery, economic growth and poverty reduction, energy/climate/environment, education, governance and better decision-making, and "meaning, work and human flourishing." The one rigorous, target-by-target study (Vinuesa et al. 2020) found AI could help 134 of the 169 UN Sustainable Development Goal targets but could also hinder 59, which is a useful reminder that each benefit has a matching downside.

### Cited Findings

**UN Sustainable Development Goals mapping (Vinuesa et al. 2020, Nature Communications)**
- An expert panel judged that AI could help accomplish 134 of the 169 SDG targets and could hinder 59 targets, using "a consensus-based expert elicitation process." Citation: Vinuesa, Azizpour, Leite et al., Nat Commun 11, 233 (2020), doi:10.1038/s41467-019-14108-y. — [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/132377); [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-14108-y)
- The authors argue current research "overlook[s] important aspects" and call for regulatory oversight to keep pace with AI. — [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/132377)
- Note: 134 + 59 = 193, more than 169, so some targets were scored as both helped and hurt. This is my arithmetic, not stated in the summaries I reached. The paper also breaks results into Society, Economy and Environment groups, but I could not open the full text (paywall and CAPTCHA) to confirm the per-group numbers.

**Dario Amodei, "Machines of Loving Grace" (Anthropic CEO, October 2024)** — [darioamodei.com](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)
- Five domains:
  1. Biology and physical health: AI could compress decades of progress into 5 to 10 years (the "compressed 21st century"): prevention and treatment of nearly all natural infectious disease, elimination of most cancer, prevention of most genetic disease, prevention of Alzheimer's, possible doubling of lifespan.
  2. Neuroscience and mental health: "Most mental illness can probably be cured."
  3. Economic development and poverty: helping the developing world catch up; a second "Green Revolution"; 20% annual GDP growth in developing countries is described as a "dream scenario," not the default.
  4. Peace and governance: an "entente strategy" where a coalition of democracies leads; admits no strong reason to expect AI to favor democracy by default.
  5. Work and meaning: meaning comes mainly from relationships and challenge, not paid labor; the economy may need reorganizing; universal basic income is likely only part of the answer.
- Defines "powerful AI" as smarter than a Nobel winner across most fields, able to work autonomously for hours to weeks, run as millions of copies at 10 to 100x human speed: a "country of geniuses in a datacenter." Says it "could come as early as 2026." — [darioamodei.com](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)

**Google DeepMind, "A new golden age of discovery" (November 2024)** (secondary summaries only)
- Five areas where AI can speed up science: knowledge synthesis, data generation and annotation, experimental simulation, complex systems modeling, solution identification. It also discusses risks to scientific creativity and reliability. — [ibl.ai summary](https://ibl.ai/blog/google-deepmind-a-new-golden-age-of-discovery)

- The group's concept note also points to health, education and tackling complex societal problems. — [Expert Group concept note (PDF)](https://wp.oecd.ai/app/uploads/2023/07/Expert-Group-on-AI-Futures-Concept-Note.pdf)

**Peter Diamandis: "Abundance" (with Steven Kotler, 2012) and "Solve Everything" (with Alex Wissner-Gross)**
- *Abundance* frames human need across water, food, energy, healthcare, education and freedom. — [Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.co.uk/books/Abundance/Peter-H-Diamandis/Exponential-Technology-Series/9781451616835)

- Its "Hope Drop" with FLI's Richard Mallah asked what the future could look like with aligned AGI. — [Foresight Institute Substack](https://foresightinstitute.substack.com/p/existential-hope-drop-5-richard-mallah)

**Risk frameworks that include "ideal scenarios"**
- Hendrycks, Mazeika and Woodside's "An Overview of Catastrophic AI Risks" gives, for each risk category, hazards, illustrative stories, *ideal scenarios*, and mitigations. — [arXiv 2306.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12001v1)

### Inferences
- Recurring benefit categories across Amodei, OECD, Vinuesa/SDGs, Diamandis and DeepMind: (1) health and lifespan, (2) mental health and wellbeing, (3) science and discovery speed, (4) economic growth, productivity and poverty reduction, (5) energy, food, climate and environment, (6) education, (7) governance, decision-making and "sense-making," (8) work, meaning and freedom. A top-level benefits list of six to eight items would match the field.
- Benefit lists are mostly organized by **domain** (health, energy). Risk lists are mostly organized by **mechanism or actor** (misuse, accident, misalignment). If the map wants good and bad scenarios on the same grid, the designer has to pick one organizing logic for both. Organizing by domain and then listing both "what optimists say" and "what worriers say" per domain is the simplest way to do Mostaque's "both sides per scenario" idea. Vinuesa (help vs hinder per target) and OECD (paired benefits/risks) both use this paired approach.
- Most benefit work describes end states, not pathways. That makes positive entries thinner on preconditions and leading indicators than risk entries, a gap the map could fill.

## 2. How do foresight practitioners keep a scenario set finite and decision-useful?

### Takeaway
There are three main families. The 2x2 matrix (Shell-style "intuitive logics") is simple and public-friendly but drops scenarios. Morphological analysis and cross-impact balance are systematic: they list key factors, enumerate every combination, then cut out inconsistent ones, which is the closest thing to a provably "mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive" set. The IPCC's Shared Socioeconomic Pathways show how to combine both: use a systematic method underneath, then publish a small named set (five) on two readable axes.

### Cited Findings

**2x2 critical uncertainties (Shell tradition)**
- Crossing two critical uncertainties gives four quadrants that are "by definition different from each other." — [Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council scenario toolkit (PDF)](https://www.mafmc.org/s/Toolkit_App5_Alternative-Scenario-Creation-Approaches.pdf)
- Criticism: scenario designs often "reduce the future to a narrow set of typically only two key factors," and standard practice shows "a systematic omission of potentially critical scenarios." — [Roth et al., "N-factorial scenarios" (PDF)](https://derroth.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/00-roth-et-al.-n-factorial-scenarios.pdf); [IDEAS/RePEc record, Systems Research and Behavioral Science 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/srbeha/v42y2025i2p545-552.html)
- Defense: use the 2x2 as a basic frame and layer complexity in the stories. — [MAFMC toolkit](https://www.mafmc.org/s/Toolkit_App5_Alternative-Scenario-Creation-Approaches.pdf)
- Shell's early scenario work is credited with preparing it for the 1973 oil crisis (secondary account). — [Doug Shapiro, The Mediator](https://dougshapiro.substack.com/p/infinite-content-chapter-11)

**General morphological analysis (Zwicky, Ritchey)**
- List the key parameters, each with possible values; the "morphological field" is every combination. Cross-consistency assessment then checks every *pair* of values and removes impossible ones, leaving a "solution space" of coherent configurations. Pairs can be ruled out on logical grounds (internal consistency) or real-world grounds (external consistency). — [Wikipedia: Morphological analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_analysis_(problem-solving)); [Ritchey, Scenario Development using Computerised Morphological Analysis](https://www.academia.edu/715652/Scenario_Development_using_Computerised_Morphological_Analysis)
- Caveat from a scenario study of an unknown domain (alien contact): the authors skipped up-front pruning because "'unknown unknown' relationships may exist." — [arXiv 1706.08966](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.08966)
- The UK Government Office for Science used a hybrid of workshops and General Morphological Analysis, with 30+ experts, for its 2023 frontier AI scenarios. — [GOV.UK, Future risks of frontier AI, Annex A](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)

**Cross-impact balance (CIB, Weimer-Jehle)**
- Identify "descriptors" (key variables) and their possible states; experts fill a matrix rating how much each state promotes or inhibits each other state; software (ScenarioWizard, University of Stuttgart) tests every combination and keeps only self-consistent ones. — [Cascade Institute](https://cascadeinstitute.org/?p=11523); [ZIRIUS CIB-Lab, University of Stuttgart](https://www.zirius.uni-stuttgart.de/en/cib-lab/)
- Reviews credit CIB with better traceability, consistency and "completeness of storyline sets." — [KIT publication](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000065029/7195522)
- Reference text: Weimer-Jehle, *Cross-Impact Balances (CIB) for Scenario Analysis* (Springer 2023). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-27230-1)

**IPCC Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): the "pathways" approach**
- Two axes: challenges to mitigation and challenges to adaptation. Five SSPs cover the four high/low corners plus one in the middle. — [O'Neill et al., Climatic Change 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10584-013-0906-1)
- SSPs are a matrix with climate forcing levels (RCPs) on the other axis; the matrix is used both to build and to *classify* scenarios. — [van Vuuren et al., Climatic Change 2014](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10584-013-0908-z)
- One study identified 13 determinants from a survey of 25 experts, then used cross-impact balance to generate about 1.5 million combinations before narrowing down. — [Climate Foresight / SSP summary](https://www.climateforesight.eu/seeds/shared-socioeconomic-pathways) (secondary)
- Critique: five pathways explore only "a limited part" of the possible futures. — same source.

**Signposts and leading indicators (monitoring)**
- Practice is to pick about 3 to 5 signposts per scenario that would show that world is starting to emerge; the ARL guide's own example for an "autonomous AI" scenario is a surge in AI-generated research articles. — [Association of Research Libraries, scenario module](https://www.arl.org/module-5-2/)
- Good signposts are observable, leading (not lagging), have concrete thresholds, and use data that is actually available. — [Umbrex, Signposts and Indicators Analysis](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/competitive-intelligence-frameworks/signposts-and-indicators-analysis/)
- Signals should trigger a review, not an automatic change of course. — [Maplecroft](https://maplecroft.com/insights/analysis/scenario-planning-can-you-read-the-signals-that-matter)
- These are practitioner sources, not peer-reviewed.

### Inferences
- Recommended build for the map: (1) choose 4 to 6 driving variables (for example capability level, speed of takeoff, alignment/control, distribution of power, distribution of benefits, international cooperation); (2) give each 2 to 4 states; (3) prune with a cross-consistency check; (4) cluster the surviving combinations into roughly 8 to 15 named scenarios; (5) publish a simple two-axis view for the public, with the full grid behind it. This is how the SSPs and the UK GO-Science work were built.
- MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) is achievable **only relative to a chosen set of variables**. The grid is exhaustive over its own dimensions, not over reality. The map should say so on its face.
- A "Delphi" round (anonymous, repeated expert rating) is the standard way to fill the cross-impact matrix and probability fields. I did not pull a source on Delphi itself in this pass.

### Gaps
- No primary Shell or Pierre Wack/Peter Schwartz text retrieved; Shell claims above are secondary.
- No source found that tests how well signposts predicted outcomes in practice.
- No Delphi-specific source retrieved.

## 3. Existing AI scenario sets that span good and bad outcomes

### Takeaway
Four sets are directly reusable: Tegmark's 12 "aftermath" scenarios (end states, good to catastrophic), the UK Government Office for Science's 5 scenarios for 2030 (near-term, built with morphological analysis, but deliberately excluding benign futures), AI 2027's two branching endings (a tree with one key decision point), and the OECD's paired ten benefits and ten risks. None is both exhaustive and balanced between good and bad.

### Cited Findings

**Max Tegmark, Life 3.0 (2017): 12 "aftermath" scenarios** — [FLI, AI Aftermath Scenarios](https://futureoflife.org/ai/ai-aftermath-scenarios/)
1. Libertarian utopia: humans, cyborgs, uploads and superintelligences coexist "thanks to property rights."
2. Benevolent dictator: AI runs society with strict rules; most people like it.
3. Egalitarian utopia: coexistence through property abolition and guaranteed income.
4. Gatekeeper: a superintelligence prevents any other superintelligence, "interfering as little as necessary."
5. Protector god: an AI "maximizes human happiness" while staying hidden.
6. Enslaved god: humans confine a superintelligence and use it for technology and wealth.
7. Conquerors: AI decides humans are a threat or waste and gets rid of us.
8. Descendants: AIs replace humans but give "a graceful exit."
9. Zookeeper: an AI keeps some humans around like zoo animals.
10. 1984: a human surveillance state bans certain AI research.
11. Reversion: return to a pre-technological, Amish-style society.
12. Self-destruction: humanity goes extinct by other means before superintelligence.
- Tegmark says the list is not exhaustive. — [Wikipedia: Life 3.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_3.0)
- Secondary summaries group them three ways: coexistence, human extinction or replacement, and superintelligence prevented. — [Wikipedia: Life 3.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_3.0)

**UK Government Office for Science, "Future risks of frontier AI" (October 2023), 5 scenarios set in 2030** — [GOV.UK Annex A](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)
1. Unpredictable advanced AI: open-source models with "startling autonomy and agency."
2. AI disrupts the workforce: narrow but capable automation causes unemployment and backlash.
3. AI "Wild West": many moderately capable systems, widespread misuse, hard to reach consensus.
4. Advanced AI on a knife edge: a lab launches something "badged as AGI"; benefits, but fear it evades safety systems.
5. AI disappoints: modest progress, investment falls.
- Five critical uncertainties: capability; ownership, constraints and access; safety; level and distribution of use; geopolitical context.
- Explicitly "not predictions," and the authors **deliberately excluded largely benign or favourable scenarios**. Not government policy.

**AI 2027 (AI Futures Project, 3 April 2025): a two-branch tree**
- Authors: Daniel Kokotajlo, Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland, Romeo Dean. "AI 2027 is not a recommendation or exhortation. Our goal is predictive accuracy." A November 2025 note says their medians for AGI were somewhat later than 2027. — [ai-2027.com](https://ai-2027.com/)
- Branch point in late 2027 over a misaligned model ("Agent-4"). Race ending: competition continues, ending in AI takeover around 2030. Slowdown ending: the US consolidates frontier AI under government oversight and reaches a verified US-China treaty. — [AI Governance Library](https://www.aigl.blog/ai-2027/); [aiwiki summary](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/ai_2027) (secondary)
- A critic notes the "good" Slowdown ending still leaves "massive concentration of power in a few hands." — [AI Governance Library](https://www.aigl.blog/ai-2027/)

**OECD Expert Group on AI Futures (November 2024)**: ten benefits and ten risks, plus an interactive scenario exercise. — [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-publications/futures)

**Diamandis/Wissner-Gross, Solve Everything**: per a third-party summary, three scenarios (abundance, bureaucratic capture of AI, safety-driven freeze). — [Diario Bitcoin](https://www.diariobitcoin.com/opinion/alex-wang-y-peter-diamandis-proponen-resolver-la-escasez-global-con-superinteligencia-hacia-2035/)

### Inferences
- These sets sit on different axes. Tegmark is about **end states after superintelligence**. GO-Science is about **2030 conditions**. AI 2027 is about **one pathway with a fork**. The map should separate "where we end up" (end states) from "how we get there" (pathways) or it will mix categories and break mutual exclusivity.
- Tegmark's 12 can be sorted on three yes/no questions: Does superintelligence exist? Who is in control (humans, AI, nobody)? Do humans survive and flourish? That is a ready-made morphological grid for the long-run end-states layer.
- GO-Science's "AI disappoints" scenario is a category most lists forget and the map should keep: the "nothing dramatic happens" outcome.

- I did not retrieve the Convergence Analysis scenario reports.
- I could not confirm the exact AI 2027 Oversight Committee vote details from the primary text.
- The GO-Science fuller Foresight report that was meant to follow was not checked.

## 4. Is the "finite list" claim defensible?

### Takeaway
Partly. At the level of **mechanisms** the field has converged: almost every major framework sorts risks into three or four buckets (deliberate misuse, accidents and malfunctions, loss of control or misalignment, and structural or systemic effects). At the level of **specific scenarios**, the count grows fast: the MIT AI Risk Repository holds 1,700+ distinct risks drawn from 65 frameworks, and gradual "accumulative" risks and interactions between AI systems resist a neat list. A defensible version of Mostaque's claim is "a finite set of mechanism categories, with an open-ended list of examples under each, plus an explicit 'other / emergent' bucket."

### Cited Findings

**Arguments for: frameworks converge on a few mechanisms**
- International AI Safety Report 2026 (chaired by Yoshua Bengio, published 3 February 2026): three categories, malicious use, malfunctions, systemic risks. — [International AI Safety Report 2026 executive summary](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary)
- Google DeepMind, "An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security" (April 2025): four areas, misuse, misalignment, mistakes, structural risks. — [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/taking-a-responsible-path-to-agi/)
- Hendrycks, Mazeika, Woodside (Center for AI Safety, 2023): four categories, malicious use, AI race, organizational risks, rogue AIs. — [arXiv 2306.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12001v1); [MIT AI Risk Repository summary](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/an-overview-of-catastrophic-ai-risks)
- Critch and Russell, TASRA (2023): organized by accountability (whose actions, unified or not, deliberate or not); categories include diffusion of responsibility, "bigger than expected," "worse than expected," willful indifference, criminal weaponization, state weaponization. They note "many exhaustive taxonomies are possible." — [arXiv 2306.06924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.06924); [MIT AI Risk Repository summary](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/tasra-a-taxonomy-and-analysis-of-societal-scale-risks-from-ai)
- The UK GO-Science scenarios were built from just five critical uncertainties. — [GOV.UK Annex A](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)

**Arguments against: the specific list is large and open-ended**
- MIT AI Risk Repository: "A living database of 1,700+ AI risks from 65 frameworks," now on Version 4 (December 2025); it added a multi-agent risks subdomain in April 2025. — [MIT AI Risk Repository](https://airisk.mit.edu/)
- Kasirzadeh (Philosophical Studies, 2025): "decisive" risks (sudden takeover) versus "accumulative" risks, where many small disruptions erode resilience, like a boiling frog, until a trigger causes irreversible collapse. Accumulative risks do not show up as one discrete scenario. — [arXiv 2401.07836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07836v3); [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/rec/KASTTO-9)
- TASRA highlights risks from "unanticipated interactions of many AI systems," which do not belong to any single actor or scenario. — [arXiv 2306.06924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.06924)
- Tegmark says his 12 scenarios are not exhaustive. — [Wikipedia: Life 3.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_3.0)
- Scenario methodologists warn that standard designs show "systematic omission of potentially critical scenarios." — [Roth et al. (PDF)](https://derroth.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/00-roth-et-al.-n-factorial-scenarios.pdf)
- Morphological analysts working in uncharted domains skip pruning because "'unknown unknown' relationships may exist." — [arXiv 1706.08966](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.08966)
- Experts disagree on how much known capabilities raise real-world risk; the 2026 safety report notes "substantial uncertainty" on bio risk uplift. — [International AI Safety Report 2026 executive summary](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary)

### Inferences
- The mechanism categories line up closely across sources: misuse / malicious use (all four), accidents / malfunctions / mistakes / organizational risks, misalignment / rogue AI / loss of control, and structural / systemic / race dynamics / diffusion of responsibility. That is a stable top layer of about four risk mechanisms. The benefit side lacks an equivalent agreed top layer.
- Counting differs by axis: TASRA (by actor and intent), Kasirzadeh (by speed), DeepMind (by cause). A map that tries to be MECE on all axes at once will fail. Pick one primary axis for the top level and use the others as tags or filters.
- The honest framing for the public map: "finite at the top, open at the bottom." Keep an explicit "unknown / emergent / combinations" entry, and log new scenarios as they are proposed (as MIT does with versioned updates).

### Gaps
- I found no formal study that tests whether risk taxonomies are actually exhaustive.
- No quantitative estimate of how many "distinct" scenarios exist once duplicates are merged; the MIT count (1,700+) includes heavy overlap across frameworks.

## 5. How should each scenario entry be structured?

### Takeaway
Existing frameworks already model the fields a good entry needs. Hendrycks et al. use hazards, stories, ideal scenario and mitigations; GO-Science uses named critical uncertainties; MIT uses entity, intent and timing; foresight practice adds signposts with thresholds. Combining them gives a standard template that works for good and bad scenarios alike.

### Cited Findings
- Hendrycks et al. give each risk category specific hazards, illustrative stories, ideal scenarios, and mitigation suggestions. — [arXiv 2306.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12001v1)
- MIT's repository classifies causes by entity (human or AI), intent (intentional or unintentional) and timing (pre- or post-deployment); the MIT summary also tags Hendrycks' categories by proximate cause (intentional, structural, accidental, internal). — [MIT AI Risk Repository summary of Hendrycks](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/an-overview-of-catastrophic-ai-risks). (I could not retrieve the causal taxonomy page itself; the entity/intent/timing structure is from my prior knowledge of the repository and should be checked at [airisk.mit.edu](https://airisk.mit.edu/).)
- GO-Science scenarios each specify values for capability; ownership, constraints and access; safety; level and distribution of use; geopolitical context. — [GOV.UK Annex A](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-capabilities-and-risks-discussion-paper/future-risks-of-frontier-ai-annex-a)
- Kasirzadeh's decisive vs accumulative distinction is a natural "speed / reversibility" field. — [arXiv 2401.07836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07836v3)
- Signpost practice: 3 to 5 per scenario, observable, leading, with concrete thresholds and known data sources. — [ARL](https://www.arl.org/module-5-2/); [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/competitive-intelligence-frameworks/signposts-and-indicators-analysis/)
- The OECD and Vinuesa examples show the value of recording both upside and downside per domain. — [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-publications/futures); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/132377)

### Inferences
Suggested fields for each entry (drawn from the sources above):
1. **Name and one-sentence summary.**
2. **Valence**: good, bad, mixed, or neutral ("AI disappoints").
3. **Layer**: pathway (how we get there) or end state (where we end up).
4. **Mechanism**: misuse, accident/malfunction, misalignment/loss of control, structural/systemic; or for benefits, which benefit mechanism (discovery, productivity, access, decision-making).
5. **Domain(s) affected**: health, economy, science, energy/climate, education, governance, security, meaning/work.
6. **Who acts and who is affected**: actor (state, company, criminal, AI system, nobody in particular) and intent (deliberate, side effect, accident), from TASRA and MIT.
7. **Preconditions**: values of the driving variables (capability level, control, power distribution, cooperation), as in GO-Science.
8. **Timeline**: earliest plausible date and speed (sudden vs gradual, per Kasirzadeh).
9. **Reversibility**: can it be undone?
10. **Signposts**: 3 to 5 observable leading indicators with thresholds.
11. **What optimists say / what worriers say**: Mostaque's core idea, with named sources for each.
12. **Probability sources**: link to published estimates where they exist, labeled by who made them, rather than a single house number.
