# Forecasting and disagreement: how AI future scenarios get probabilities, and how the worried vs. optimistic gap has been measured

Research notes for the "AI scenario map" project. Current as of 9 October 2026. Dates are given for every study because the numbers age fast. Source citations follow each claim in brackets.

## 1. The Forecasting Research Institute's Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament (XPT) and its follow-ups

### Takeaway
The best-designed attempt to measure the worried vs. skeptical gap (2022) found domain experts roughly 8 times more worried than top-track-record forecasters about AI extinction by 2100 (3% vs. 0.38%). Four months of structured, incentivized debate did not narrow the gap, and a 2023 follow-up built specifically to make the two camps engage ("adversarial collaboration") also produced almost no movement. Where the gap did shrink was on very long horizons (year 2500+), where both camps put serious negative outcomes at 30-40%.

### Cited Findings
**Method**
- XPT ran in 2022 with 80 domain experts and 89 superforecasters (169 total). It was "a multi-stage tournament designed to incentivize forecasts, explanations, persuasion, and updating," covering AI, nuclear war, pathogens and other threats over short, medium and long horizons. [FRI XPT page](https://forecastingresearch.org/xpt). (Note: one wiki summary reverses the 80/89 split; FRI's own page is the one to trust. [Longterm Wiki](https://www.longtermwiki.com/wiki/xpt))
- Forecasters stopped updating on 31 October 2022, so all XPT numbers predate ChatGPT and GPT-4. [EA Forum XPT results series](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/s/b3AtJeBggrjfDKCyj); [Astral Codex Ten, 20 Jul 2023](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-extinction-tournament)
- The peer-reviewed version was published in the International Journal of Forecasting in 2025, with authors from FRI, the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago and Wharton. [Longterm Wiki XPT results summary](https://www.longtermwiki.com/resources/5c91c25b0c337e1b) (secondary; journal citation not independently checked). Working paper PDF: [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf)

**Headline numbers (medians, by 2100)**
- AI-caused human extinction: domain experts 3%, superforecasters 0.38%. [Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-extinction-tournament); [Narayanan & Kapoor, 26 Jul 2024](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)
- All-cause catastrophe: experts about 20%, superforecasters 9%. All-cause extinction: experts about 6%, superforecasters 1%. [Longterm Wiki XPT summary](https://www.longtermwiki.com/resources/5c91c25b0c337e1b) (secondary)
- Superforecasters put AI catastrophe at about 2.13% by 2100, lower than their nuclear catastrophe estimate (4%), though they rated AI extinction above nuclear extinction. [Longterm Wiki](https://www.longtermwiki.com/wiki/xpt) (secondary). I could not confirm the experts' AI-catastrophe median from a primary source.
- The two groups disagreed most on AI and least on nuclear war. [Longterm Wiki XPT summary](https://www.longtermwiki.com/resources/5c91c25b0c337e1b)
- Spread inside each camp is enormous: Narayanan and Kapoor note the 75th-percentile AI expert and 25th-percentile superforecaster differ by at least a factor of 100. [Narayanan & Kapoor](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)
- People who were worried about AI also tended to be worried about bio and nuclear risk (risk estimates were correlated across topics), and forecasters who were best at predicting what others would say gave lower risk numbers across the board. [Longterm Wiki XPT summary](https://www.longtermwiki.com/resources/5c91c25b0c337e1b)

**Did discussion narrow the gap?**
- No. FRI's abstract reports "large-scale disagreement and minimal convergence of beliefs," and calls the lack of convergence the most puzzling scientific question raised. [FRI XPT page](https://forecastingresearch.org/xpt)
- Scott Alexander's read: the two groups agreed on easier, data-backed questions, so "This is a real, strong disagreement," not a competence gap. He points to the crux of whether AI progress reflects general intelligence or narrow optimization, and how powerful AI will get. [Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-extinction-tournament)
- Incentive critique: you can't be rewarded for correctly predicting extinction, which may distort tail-risk forecasts. [Longterm Wiki XPT summary](https://www.longtermwiki.com/resources/5c91c25b0c337e1b); Alexander considers this and concludes "we're not going to escape that easily." [Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-extinction-tournament)

**Adversarial collaboration follow-up ("Roots of Disagreement on AI Risk")**
- 22 participants, April-May 2023. The "concerned" group (all domain experts) gave a 20% chance of AI-caused existential catastrophe by 2100; the "skeptical" group (mostly superforecasters) gave 0.12%. [FRI post via EA Forum mirror](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri); full report at forecastingresearch.org/s/AIcollaboration.pdf (not opened)
- "Neither the concerned nor the skeptics substantially updated toward the other's views." [same](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)
- Each side named "cruxes": questions resolving by 2030 that would most change their 2100 view. The top crux was expected to close only about 5% of the gap. [same](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)
- Over a 1,000-year horizon, agreement was much higher: medians of 30% (skeptics) and 40% (concerned) for severe negative effects on humanity. [same](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)
- Recruitment caveat: the concerned group came through referrals from Open Philanthropy staff (the funder) and the effective altruism community. [same](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/orhjaZ3AJMHzDzckZ/results-from-an-adversarial-collaboration-on-ai-risk-fri)
- Published academically in Risk Analysis (FRI post dated 3 April 2025, Rosenberg, Karger, Jacobs, Hickman, Morris, Durland, Kuusela, Tetlock). Two studies; neither group changed long-term estimates; disagreement "persistent at 2100" but shrank toward 2500+. [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/belief-updating-in-ai-risk-debates-exploring-the-limits-of-adversarial-collaboration)

**Near-term accuracy check (2 Sep 2025)**
- Both groups "misjudged the pace and direction of AI progress," and both underestimated it. Superforecasters were more wrong. [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy)
- Gold-medal performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad by 2025: experts gave 8.6%, superforecasters 2.3%. It happened in July 2025, about five years before the median expert forecast and ten before the median superforecaster forecast. [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy)
- Across four AI benchmarks, superforecasters averaged 9.7% and experts 24.6% on what actually happened. Caveats: differences not statistically significant; only 38 resolved subquestions. [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy)

**LEAP: the Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (launched Nov 2025, ongoing)**
- Monthly forecasts from 339 experts (76 computer scientists, 76 industry, 68 economists, 119 policy researchers) plus superforecasters, planned for three years. [LEAP paper copy](https://etcjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8fc30-the-longitudinal-expert-ai-panel.pdf); [FRI LEAP page](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/longitudinal-expert-ai-panel-leap); [HPCwire, 13 Nov 2025](https://www.hpcwire.com/2025/11/13/new-forecasting-study-gauges-expert-expectations-for-ai/)
- Median expert: AI about 7% of US electricity by 2030, assisting 18% of US work hours, daily companionship for 15% of adults; 23% chance of very rapid "AGI-like" progress by 2030; most forecasts "fall far short" of lab leaders' warnings about imminent superintelligence. [LEAP paper copy](https://etcjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8fc30-the-longitudinal-expert-ai-panel.pdf)
- Useful for the map: within-person uncertainty and between-person disagreement each account for about half of total variation. [LEAP paper copy](https://etcjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8fc30-the-longitudinal-expert-ai-panel.pdf)
- A critic found one scenario question reported vote-share predictions as if they were probabilities; FRI changed the wording. [EA Forum comment thread](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/PjGRFxXrGENQTTkWm/introducing-leap-the-longitudinal-expert-ai-panel/comment/9gtykSun9fAJcBfg9)

### Inferences
- The XPT gap is not a "who is smarter" gap. The worried side was more accurate on near-term AI capability questions, while the skeptic side had better track records in general. A map should show both facts side by side rather than declaring a winner.
- Debate alone does not move people. The one lever that does seem to work is agreeing in advance on near-term "crux" questions that will resolve. Those make excellent "what would change our mind" fields for each scenario.
- The sharp convergence at very long horizons (30-40% bad outcomes by 2500 for both camps) suggests a big part of the fight is about speed, not about whether danger exists. That is worth surfacing.
- All XPT numbers are from 2022. Any map that shows them should carry a visible "as of" date.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full XPT PDF (file too large for the fetch tool), so per-horizon (2030/2050) AI numbers and the expert AI-catastrophe median are unverified.
- The January 2025 FRI "hybrid persuasion-forecasting tournament" exists ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/subjective-probability-forecasts-of-existential-risk-initial-results-from-a-hybrid-persuasion-forecasting-tournament)) but I did not read its results.
- I did not find the specific list of 2030 cruxes from the adversarial collaboration.
- No LEAP waves after the first report were checked.

## 2. Metaculus, Manifold, Good Judgment and other forecasting platforms

### Takeaway
Crowd platforms carry many AI questions (AGI dates, Turing-test style milestones, benchmarks), shown as live probability distributions rather than single numbers. Their measurable track record on AI is "better than a coin flip, worse than their average," and nobody can score their extinction forecasts.

### Cited Findings
- Metaculus is free and reputation-based (no money at stake). For date questions, forecasters submit a full probability distribution, not a single year; "When will AGI be publicly demonstrated?" is a standard example. [pm.wiki explainer](https://pm.wiki/es/learn/metaculus-explained) (secondary)
- A third-party snapshot (July 2026) reports roughly 1,800 Metaculus forecasters putting the first "weakly general AI" at 2028, about 180 putting a long adversarial Turing test pass at 2029, and over 1,100 Manifold traders putting a high-quality adversarial Turing test at 2035. These are different questions and should not be merged. [AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/specialized-ai) (secondary; live numbers not checked)
- Metaculus's 2021 self-analysis of AI questions: 64 resolved binary questions, Community Prediction Brier score 0.207 vs. 0.25 for always guessing 50%; continuous questions scored better (CRPS 0.096 vs. 0.172 baseline). [EA Forum / Metaculus notebook](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/e9htD7txe8RDdcehm/exploring-metaculus-s-ai-track-record)
- A 2023 critique found Metaculus did worse on (some) AI questions than its overall average; Metaculus replied the critique used only a subset. Scores also shift depending on when forecasts are evaluated. [EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/e9htD7txe8RDdcehm/exploring-metaculus-s-ai-track-record); [EA Forum, community predictions](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/zeL52MFB2Pkq9Kdme/exploring-metaculus-community-predictions)
- In February 2026 Metaculus launched FutureEval to score AI forecasting bots; human Pro Forecasters have kept the lead. [Metaculus FutureEval methodology](https://www.metaculus.com/futureeval/methodology/)
- Narayanan and Kapoor's structural point: scoring rules can't measure skill on one-off events like extinction, and are "insensitive to overestimating tail risks." [Narayanan & Kapoor](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)

### Inferences
- Platform numbers are most trustworthy for near-term, checkable milestones and least trustworthy for "doom" questions, which can never pay out. The map could label each probability by whether it is "scoreable" or "not scoreable."
- Showing a live distribution (with the date pulled) is better than quoting a median, since platform medians move by years within months.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable data on Good Judgment Open's AI questions or on Manifold's AI track record.
- I did not check current live Metaculus or Manifold numbers for AGI or AI-catastrophe questions.

## 3. AI Impacts / Katja Grace expert surveys

### Takeaway
The 2023 "Thousands of AI Authors" survey (2,778 researchers, fall 2023) found a median 5% (mean 9-16%, depending on wording) chance of extremely bad outcomes such as human extinction, with a long right tail. The answers move with wording: asking about "inability to control" AI doubled the median to 10%. No new 2025-2026 survey wave was found, only a revised paper (Oct 2025).

### Cited Findings
- 2,778 researchers who published at six top AI venues; survey ran in fall 2023; response rate 15% (2,778 of 18,459 working emails). The authors tested for response bias and "did not find any." [Grace et al., PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)
- Paper versions: v1 5 Jan 2024, v2 30 Apr 2024, v3 8 Oct 2025; published in Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 84:9 (2025). [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)
- "Extremely bad outcomes (e.g. human extinction)" from high-level machine intelligence: median 5%, mean 9%; 38% put at least 10%; one in ten put at least 25%. [Grace et al., PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)
- Three wordings of the extinction question (2023 results): "AI advances causing human extinction or similarly permanent and severe disempowerment": median 5%, mean 16.2% (n=1,321). "Human inability to control future advanced AI systems causing" the same: median 10%, mean 19.4% (n=661). Same as first, "within the next 100 years": median 5%, mean 14.4% (n=655). Depending on wording, 41.2% to 51.4% gave more than 10%. [Grace et al., PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)
- Mixed views are normal: 68.3% thought good outcomes more likely than bad, yet nearly half of these optimists (48%) gave at least 5% to extremely bad outcomes, and 59% of pessimists gave at least 5% to extremely good outcomes. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)
- Change since 2022: share giving at least 10% to extremely bad outcomes fell from 48% to 38%, and the mean fell from 14% to 9%. [Grace et al., PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)
- Known framing effects on timelines: "high-level machine intelligence" (all tasks) at 50% by 2047, but "full automation of labor" (all occupations) at 50% by 2116, a 60-70 year gap for nearly the same event. Authors: "the difference ... would seem to be a framing effect." Also, asking "probability by year X" consistently gives later dates than asking "year by which probability Y." [Grace et al., PDF](https://aiimpacts.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Thousands_of_AI_authors_on_the_future_of_AI.pdf)
- Press coverage misreported the figures (e.g., "almost 58%" in Fox station stories vs. 48% in the paper) and framed the same survey both as alarming and reassuring. [Fox 6](https://www.fox6now.com/news/ai-chance-of-human-extinction-survey); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/211923/transformed-not-terminated-ai-researchers-weigh-in-on-the-future?amp=1)
- Critique: Narayanan and Kapoor argue such surveys suffer non-response bias and shared biases and are "misinformative and should be ignored by policymakers." [Narayanan & Kapoor](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)

### Inferences
- The median (5%) and mean (9-19%) tell very different stories because a minority give very high numbers. A map should show the full distribution (for example, a dot or histogram plot), never just one summary number.
- The wording swing (5% vs. 10% median) is itself a public-communication lesson: tiny wording changes in a scenario title can double the number people attach to it. The map should publish the exact question wording next to any number.
- The fact that most optimists still give non-trivial weight to catastrophe, and most pessimists give non-trivial weight to utopia, undercuts the "two tribes" picture. Worth showing.

### Gaps
- No 2025 or 2026 AI Impacts survey wave found. The October 2025 arXiv revision appears to be a revision of the 2023 data, not new data; I did not confirm what changed.

## 4. Scenario narratives: AI 2027, "AI as Normal Technology," Situational Awareness, Epoch AI

### Takeaway
The two most-cited narratives disagree mainly about speed and about whether more capability automatically means more real-world power. In November 2025 the authors of AI 2027 and AI as Normal Technology wrote a joint essay mapping where they agree, which is the closest existing thing to the "worried and optimistic in their own words" format the map wants. AI 2027's authors have since pushed their dates back.

### Cited Findings
- Joint essay "Common Ground between AI 2027 & AI as Normal Technology" (Asterisk, Nov 2025). Shared claims: before strong AGI, AI is a normal technology; high benchmark scores do not equal real-world automation; long-tail errors may keep AI out of high-assurance settings through at least 2029. Shared policy priorities include stopping AI from autonomously controlling critical systems, developer transparency, independent auditing, and preventing unmonitored recursive self-improvement. [Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/common-ground-between-ai-2027-and); [PDF copy](https://pdf.stafforini.com/kapoor-2025-common-ground-between.pdf)
- Core split: AI 2027 expects transformation within a decade (up to superintelligence); the normal-technology view expects electricity- or internet-style gradual change, treats diffusion and friction as central, and sees AGI as a property of system plus environment. [Asterisk](https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/common-ground-between-ai-2027-and); [commentary, dkalish](https://dkalish.substack.com/i/163003328/analysis)
- One commentary frames the root difference as whether "more capable" models inevitably become "more powerful" ones. [dkalish](https://dkalish.substack.com/i/163003328/analysis) (secondary interpretation)
- Narayanan and Kapoor: their point is not "nothing to see here." [AI as Normal Technology guide](https://www.normaltech.ai/i/173147197/concluding-thoughts)
- AI 2027 revisions: "2027" was the authors' most likely single year (mode), not the median. A 31 December 2025 model update pushed full coding automation out about 3-5 years; early-2026 medians for superintelligence ranged from 2031 (Kokotajlo) to the mid-2030s (Lifland). The authors say media compared old modes to new medians and overstated the shift. [Wikipedia, AI Futures Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Futures_Project); [Capacity](https://capacityglobal.com/news/former-openai-researcher-pushes-back-agi-timeline/). Wikipedia also reports an April 2026 note saying coding-tool progress looked closer to the original scenario, so direction is not settled.
- The AI Futures Project published "AI 2040: Plan A" on 9 July 2026. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Futures_Project)
- Situational Awareness (Aschenbrenner, June 2024): critiques call its AGI-by-2027 case dependent on "load-bearing leaps of faith" [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/ai-timelines-and-national-security--the-obstacles-to-agi-by-2027) and note he is an AI investor, "not a disinterested party." [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/06/23/leopold-aschenbrenner-ai-future-silicon-valley)
- Epoch AI's GATE model tries to bridge economists (Acemoglu: under 2% GDP boost over ten years) and those expecting 30%+ annual growth; Epoch says GATE is for "qualitative insights, not ... precise quantitative predictions." [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/ai-and-explosive-growth-redux). Epoch's own researchers disagree on timelines. [Epoch, Future of AI](https://epoch.ai/topics/future-of-ai)

### Inferences
- The joint essay is a ready-made template: one section of shared ground, one of named disagreements, one of shared policy. The map can copy that structure per scenario.
- Policy convergence is greater than forecast convergence. Even camps with very different numbers agreed on several concrete policies, which supports Mostaque's triage idea (policy vs. communication) without needing to settle the probabilities first.
- Track how a narrative's own authors revise it. Publishing "what they said then, what they say now" is more honest than freezing a 2025 scenario.

### Gaps
- I found no formal, item-by-item disagreement matrix across all four narratives; the joint essay covers only two.
- I did not verify AI 2027's own grading of its 2025 predictions (a secondary source says progress ran at about 65% of their expected pace). [Medium](https://medium.com/data-science-collective/situational-awareness-two-years-later-4b941d052ef9)

## 5. Decision framing: expected utility vs. minimax regret vs. precaution vs. robust decision-making

### Takeaway
Expected utility needs agreed probabilities, which the evidence above shows we don't have for AI catastrophe. Minimax regret and the precautionary principle avoid needing probabilities but focus attention on worst cases and can give up large benefits. RAND's Robust Decision Making sidesteps the fight by asking which policies perform acceptably across many futures, which suits a map that refuses to pick one set of numbers. I found no RAND or academic study applying these frameworks directly to AI.

### Cited Findings
- Minimax regret (Savage, 1951): pick the option whose worst-case shortfall versus the best choice in each scenario is smallest; used when probabilities are unknown. [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/savage-minimax-regret/)
- Plain minimax can be hijacked by one disaster scenario where every option fails; regret scoring avoids that by weighting scenarios where the choice makes a difference. [arXiv 2203.01420](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.01420)
- Regret rules can be gamed by adding irrelevant options (they violate "independence of irrelevant alternatives"). [arXiv 2403.02960](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02960)
- Minimax regret is "maximally ambiguity averse," which may mean giving up large upside. [EA Forum, model-based policy analysis](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/kCBQHWqbk4Nrns8P7/model-based-policy-analysis-under-deep-uncertainty) (informal)
- Precautionary principle: be driven primarily by avoiding catastrophe even if that costs substantial benefits; Stewart (2025) shows precise versions run into impossibility results under deep uncertainty. [Cambridge, Philosophy of Science](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C5F5919A78917692661D5F74EC072DB9/S0031824824000400a.pdf/deep-uncertainty-and-incommensurability-general-cautions-about-precaution.pdf)
- RAND's definition of deep uncertainty: when parties "do not know, or agree on" the model linking actions to consequences or the likelihood of future events. RDM runs models "many hundreds to thousands of times" to see how plans perform across plausible futures. [RAND RB9701](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9701.html)
- RDM uses computation "not to make better predictions, but to yield better decisions" and runs as "deliberation with analysis": stakeholders set objectives, work with experts, then revisit choices. [RAND, Lempert](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP67834.html)
- Practical limit: a regret table needs a list of futures; if you can't list them, the method "breaks ... at step one." [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/minimax-regret-rule/)
- Narayanan and Kapoor's alternative: adopt policies "that help across a range of risk estimates," and make policy processes responsive to new evidence. [Narayanan & Kapoor](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)

### Inferences
- These frameworks need different inputs. Expected utility needs probabilities; minimax regret needs only a scenario list and outcomes; RDM needs scenarios plus candidate policies. A finite scenario map is exactly the input minimax regret and RDM require, which is a strong argument for the project regardless of which framework a reader prefers.
- A neutral presentation: for each policy, show (a) how it does if you trust the median numbers (expected-utility view), (b) its worst-case regret across all scenarios (minimax-regret view), and (c) how many scenarios it does "acceptably" in (RDM view). Let readers toggle lenses rather than the map choosing.
- Mostaque's point that fear stories push people toward minimax regret can be made visible: a vivid catastrophe scenario dominates the regret view even at a tiny probability. Showing both lenses side by side lets readers see the effect instead of being steered by it.

### Gaps
- No source found applying RDM, minimax regret or the precautionary principle specifically to AI scenarios. Several of the cited decision-theory pages are informal explainers rather than peer-reviewed work.

## 6. Communicating probabilities and uncertainty to the public

### Takeaway
Probability words alone get misread toward 50%. The IPCC's fix (words paired with number ranges, plus a separate "confidence" rating for evidence quality) measurably helps across languages. For AI, where numbers vary 100-fold between people, the more honest display is the spread of opinion with the question wording and date, not a single headline figure.

### Cited Findings
- IPCC likelihood scale: virtually certain 99-100%, very likely 90-100%, likely 66-100%, about as likely as not 33-66%, unlikely 0-33%, very unlikely 0-10%, exceptionally unlikely 0-1%; boundaries are "fuzzy." Likelihood is kept separate from "confidence," a qualitative rating of evidence type, amount, quality and agreement. [IPCC uncertainty guidance note](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/ipcc_uncertainty-guidance-note.pdf); [GreenFacts AR5](https://www.greenfacts.org/en/climate-change-ar5-science-basis/l-2/1-likelihood.htm)
- The terms are to be used only where the panel can actually quantify the uncertainty. [IPCC guidance note](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/ipcc_uncertainty-guidance-note.pdf)
- Budescu et al. (2014, Nature Climate Change, 24 countries, 17 languages): lay readers pull IPCC terms toward 50%; adding the numeric range next to the word improved correct interpretation and made results more consistent across languages. [Nature Climate Change via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcli/v4y2014i6d10.1038_nclimate2194.html); [ANU](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/the-interpretation-of-ipcc-probabilistic-statements-around-the-wo/)
- Most readers interpreted "very likely" as about 70%, not 90%+; the dual word-plus-number format roughly doubled correct readings for the most extreme terms. [Fordham](https://now.fordham.edu/?p=28823) (secondary)
- Even with the conversion table, 54% of interpretations still fell outside the intended range (Budescu et al. 2009). [UCL review](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1528764/3/Harris_R1%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Budescu presented this to the IPCC directly in 2016. [IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/2016/02/Budescu_IPCC_Communication_Meeting_OSLO_February_2016.pdf)
- Narayanan and Kapoor: a probability has no authority on its own; forecasters should be required to explain their methods and evidence. [Narayanan & Kapoor](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)

### Inferences
- For each scenario, the map could carry two separate badges, borrowed from the IPCC: a likelihood range (from whoever supplied it) and a confidence level about the evidence (for example, "low: no track record possible"). Most AI catastrophe numbers would honestly sit at "low confidence," which is itself useful information.
- Always show words and numbers together ("unlikely, 0-33%"), never words alone.
- Show who gave the number (superforecasters, AI researchers, platform crowd, scenario authors), the exact wording, the date, and the spread (25th-75th percentile at minimum). The XPT and AI Impacts data show the spread is the story.
- Mark which numbers can ever be scored ("checkable by 2030") versus which cannot. That directly answers the strongest critique (Narayanan and Kapoor) without excluding the numbers.

### Gaps
